\begin{abstract}
An agentic LLM server keeps a program's KV cache across tool calls, and
every KV decision asks what one miss costs. For replicas with a prefill
queue, a decode batch and one memory pool we derive the \emph{price of
a miss}, the time to first token it adds summed over all turns. Beyond
its recompute, a miss delays every prefill queued behind it, a
quadratic term that chunked prefill does not remove; the decode batch
sees only mean work. One shadow price of memory decides eviction,
offloading, placement and admission. With few sessions live the
open-queue wait is an upper bound. On an NPU testbed at 50k-token
contexts turns wait for KV blocks, not prefill compute, and the turns
that miss are those that queued; at short contexts
forced misses raise the prefill queue by an amount inside the price's
bracket, over half of it the head-of-line term.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Response time is queueing delay plus service time, $T=W_q+S$.
Hardware benchmarks measure~$S$; users experience~$T$, and near
saturation $W_q$ dominates: in the simplest Markovian model, raising
the load from $\rho=0.90$ to $0.99$ multiplies response time by
ten.\provedby{\leanref{mm1Wait\_example\_ratio}} The serving question
is therefore which control knob moves the load at which the time to
first token (TTFT) crosses its service-level target.

Agentic workloads sharpen the problem. In production coding-agent traces
the median session has \wkTurnsMedian{} LLM turns over a context of
\wkContextMedian k tokens, and almost every turn adds a tool result to
a prompt the server has already processed and may still hold in memory
(\S\ref{sec:exp-traces}; \citealp{vllm-agentx}). The unit of work is
the \emph{program}, a session with persistent KV state
\citep{thunderagent}, whose KV occupies memory while it waits for a
tool.

Every KV decision in such a system asks what it costs to lose a
paused program's state. The obvious answer is the time to recompute
it. In a queue that answer is incomplete: a miss is long work added to
a shared server, every other request pays for it, and how much depends
on the load and on whether the miss shares the batch or blocks it.
Queueing models of LLM serving have so far asked when a server is
stable and which schedulers are throughput-optimal, with output length
as the source of variability
\citep{yang2024mg1,dai2025workconserving,ao2025fluid,nie2026stability};
agentic serving systems keep or evict the KV of paused programs by
rules of thumb such as recency, time-to-live or idleness
\citep{li2025continuum,xia2026mori,thunderagent}. We connect the two.
The variability that matters here is in the prefill work, and it has
two sources: the size of the append, which the scheduler does not
control, and the hit/miss mixture that the KV policy creates, which it
does. The second can be priced per miss, and that price is the decision
variable the policy needs.

This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A formulation and the price of a miss} (\S\ref{sec:model}).
Sessions arrive as a Poisson process and loop through turns and tools.
A replica has a prefill queue that serves pending prefills in order, a
decode batch that shares bandwidth, and one memory pool. A miss changes
only the prefill work, and we derive its price at the prefill queue as
the delay one longer job adds to itself and to the jobs behind it
(Proposition~\ref{prop:price}); the decode batch depends on the work
only through its mean, so a miss leaves it unchanged
(Proposition~\ref{prop:decode}). With $N$ sessions live a waiting
session cannot submit its next turn, and for exponential work we prove
that the usual open-queue wait at the same utilisation overstates the
true wait
(Proposition~\ref{prop:finite}).
\item \textbf{Congestion-priced scheduling} (\S\ref{sec:sched}). The
scheduler compares one number per paused program, its expected price
per byte of memory per second held, with one shadow price of memory.
That comparison drives eviction, offloading, placement and admission.
Eviction is a covering knapsack whose standard solutions we cite. Any
eviction rule that ignores resume probabilities can be made arbitrarily
bad, and shortest-first is the special case in which the price is the
recompute alone (Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}); in the traces we
measured it is the pause time, not the resume probability, that
separates programs.
\item \textbf{Results} (\S\ref{sec:exp}). Production traces give the
workload side: the append alone has a prefill-work $\mathrm{CV}^2$ of
\wkCvN, resume probabilities vary little and pause times vary
widely. In simulation the admission cap, not the eviction order,
decides whether a replica thrashes (the priced order beats
shortest-first only within seed noise), and on replayed sessions under
vLLM's engine rules the open price overstates the rise from forced
misses by \simPriceOverMin{}
to \simPriceOverMax{} times. On an NPU testbed
(\S\ref{sec:exp-testbed}) the fitted prefill cost is quadratic past
\eOneKc k tokens; a replay of a coding-agent trace at 50k-token
contexts shows turns waiting for KV blocks, which the cap on live
sessions controls, and the misses among the turns that queued; and a
short-context replay, where the prefill queue binds, puts both wait
formulas within a factor of two of the observed wait and the rise from
forced misses inside the bracket of Proposition~\ref{prop:price}, over
half of it the head-of-line term and over half of it borne by turns
that hit. The remaining experiments (Appendix~\ref{app:design}) are
not yet run.
\end{enumerate}

Propositions are our results, proved in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs};
standard results are cited, not re-proved; other papers are quoted by
section and figure, and we have not re-run their experiments.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:model}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Background.} An LLM request is served in two phases. Prefill
reads the whole prompt in one pass and produces the first token; the
wait for it to start plus its duration is the time to first token
(TTFT). Decode then produces one token per step. Both phases keep the attention state of every prompt
token, the KV cache, in device memory; when a later request starts
with the same tokens, prefix caching reuses that state (a \emph{hit})
instead of recomputing it (a \emph{miss}). An engine such as vLLM
batches the decode steps of many requests and, with chunked prefill,
feeds pending prefills into those steps in fixed-size chunks
\citep{sarathi2024}. An agent program is a session of many requests,
each appending a tool result to the previous prompt, so nearly every
request could be a hit if its state survived the tool call. The memory
held by paused sessions is what the KV policy manages.

Table~\ref{tab:model} is the model on one page: sessions arrive and
loop through turns and tool calls (\S\ref{sec:sessions}); each turn is
served by one of $J$ replicas, each a prefill queue, a decode batch and
a memory pool (\S\ref{sec:batch}); the KV policy decides which turns
hit and so what the prefill queue serves (\S\ref{sec:congestion});
\S\ref{sec:price} states the decision problem and derives the one
quantity every decision in it needs.

\subsection{Sessions, Turns and Tools}\label{sec:sessions}\label{sec:closed}

An agent program, or session, cycles through
$\text{prefill}\to\text{decode}\to\text{tool}\to\text{prefill}\to\cdots$.
Sessions arrive in a Poisson process of rate~$\Lambda$; inside a
session the loop is closed: a turn is served, its tool call takes a
random time, the \emph{think time} of an interactive network
\citep{lazowska1984}, and with probability~$p_i$ the session issues
another turn. Turns reach the replicas at rate
$\lambda=\Lambda\,\E[\text{turns per session}]$. For a network of processor-sharing stations and delay stations
(stations without queueing, such as the tool call) the BCMP theorem
gives a product-form stationary law \citep{bcmp1975}: each station
behaves as if fed by Poisson arrivals at its own rate, whatever the
tool-time law. The decode stage is such a station. The FIFO prefill
stage is not, and we use the same Poisson reading for it as an
approximation, tested in \S\ref{sec:sim} and \S\ref{sec:exp-testbed}.

\subsection{A Replica: Two Resources and a Memory Pool}\label{sec:batch}\label{sec:queue}

A turn with context $K_i$ appends $n_i$ tokens and decodes $o_i$. It
makes three demands on its replica.
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Prefill compute.} Prefilling $n$ tokens onto a resident
  prefix of $K$ costs
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:prefill}
    P(n,K)=a\,n+b\,n\,(K+n/2),
  \end{equation}
  a linear term for the dense layers and an attention term over the
  prefix. A hit costs $P(n_i,K_i)$; a miss re-prefills the context,
  $P(K_i+n_i,0)$, quadratic in $K_i$, as ThunderAgent's Lemma~4.1 also
  assumes \citep{thunderagent}.
\item \emph{Decode bandwidth.} Each decode step reads the weights once
  for the batch and the KV of every turn in it, so decoding $o_i$ tokens
  takes
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:decode}
    D_i = o_i\bigl(\beta K_i+\omega/m\bigr)
  \end{equation}
  seconds of bandwidth, with $m$ the batch size, $\beta$ the time to
  read one token's KV and $\omega$ the time to read the weights. Which
  term dominates depends on the hardware (on our testbed $\beta\approx0$,
  \S\ref{sec:sim}); neither differs between a hit and a miss.
\item \emph{Memory.} $c_i=\kappa K_i$ bytes for as long as the state is
  resident, running or paused, with $\kappa$ the KV bytes per token.
\end{itemize}
The hit/miss distinction lives entirely in the first demand.

\paragraph{Decode stage.} Continuous batching advances every turn in
the batch at each iteration, so decoding turns share the device. We
model the decode stage as a processor-sharing (PS) station: a batch of
$m$ turns completes $\varphi(m)$ seconds of demand~\eqref{eq:decode} per
second, and every turn in it advances at the same rate; batching makes
$\varphi$ increase with $m$ and memory makes it saturate. The
stationary law of the batch size is
$\pi(m)\propto\rho_D^m/\prod_{k\le m}\varphi(k)$ with the decode load
$\rho_D=\lambda\E[D]$, and it is \emph{insensitive}: it depends on the
demand only through its mean \citep{bcmp1975,kelly1979}.

\paragraph{Prefill stage.} With chunked prefill each step first serves
the decode batch, then spends the tokens left in its budget on pending
prefills \citep{sarathi2024}. Chunking protects the decoding turns from
a long prefill; it does not let one prefill overtake another, and by
default the schedulers we know of serve pending prefills in arrival
order. vLLM serves its running requests in admission order rather than
decode first; the two orders coincide as long as no per-request cap
limits a prefill's tokens in a step, because a prefill that does not
finish leaves no budget for the requests behind
it.\provedby{\leanref{serve\_preserves\_shape}, \leanref{serve\_eq\_decode\_first}, \leanref{chunk\_cap\_breaks\_shape}}
We therefore model the prefill
stage as a FIFO queue whose server is available a fraction $1-\rho_D$
of the time; we write $S_i$ for the work $P_i$ of turn~$i$ measured in
that server's own time and $\rho=\lambda\E[S]$ for the prefill load.
For Poisson arrivals to a FIFO server with load $\rho<1$ and
$\E[S^2]<\infty$ (M/G/1) the Pollaczek--Khinchine (PK) formula gives
the mean wait
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pk}
  \E[W_q] = \frac{\lambda \E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)}
\end{equation}
\citep{pollaczek1930,khinchine1932}. Session feedback makes arrivals
non-Poisson and the budget fluctuates, so~\eqref{eq:pk} is an
approximation here. With $N$ sessions live the queue is a finite-source
system, and a session that is waiting cannot generate its next turn:
Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} shows that the open formula then bounds
the Markovian wait from above. The two stages thus differ in what they need
from the cache: the decode stage needs only mean demands, which the
cache does not change; the prefill stage needs the second moment of its
work, which the cache sets.

\paragraph{Memory pool.} The batch and the paused states share one
pool of $M$ bytes, so a replica admits turns into the batch until the
next one does not fit. The batch that fits is a property of the
footprint law and the admission rule, not of the mean footprint (a
more variable footprint can shrink the batch or enlarge
it),\provedby{\leanref{footprint\_variance\_hurts}, \leanref{footprint\_variance\_helps}}
so $\varphi$ is to be calibrated (Appendix~\ref{app:design}), not
derived.
A turn whose KV does not fit waits for blocks before its prefill
starts; that wait $W_q^M$ adds to the queueing wait $W_q^P$
of~\eqref{eq:pk} and is set by the pool, not by the queue. Once resident KV exceeds the pool the policy chooses
what to drop, which sets the hit rate~$h$. A shared KV store \citep{mooncake-vllm}
adds a slower tier every replica can read, so a state that is not
resident is fetched instead of recomputed.

\subsection{Prefill Work under KV Reuse}\label{sec:congestion}

With hit probability~$h$ the prefill work of a turn is the mixture
$S=S^{\mathrm{hit}}=P(n_i,K_i)$ with probability $h$ and
$S=S^{\mathrm{miss}}=P(K_i+n_i,0)$ otherwise, so the KV policy sets
$\E[S]$, $\E[S^2]$ and~$\rho$. At the FIFO prefill stage,
\eqref{eq:pk} makes the second moment a first-class quantity: of two
work laws with the same mean, the one with the larger variance waits
longer at every stable load,\provedby{\leanref{pkWait\_lt\_of\_variance\_lt}}
and raising the hit rate lowers both moments at once, so the PK wait is
non-increasing in~$h$.\provedby{\leanref{meanService\_antitone}, \leanref{utilization\_antitone}, \leanref{secondMomentService\_antitone}, \leanref{pkWait\_mixture\_antitone}}

We do not assume that agentic workloads have intrinsically high
variance; we ask where it comes from. Table~\ref{tab:cv2} splits
$\mathrm{Var}[S]$ into the spread of the appends within hits and within
misses and the hit/miss mixture between them. With every turn a hit,
as the prefix hashes of the production traces say $\wkReuse\,\%$ of
their tokens would be, the append alone gives $\mathrm{CV}^2=\wkCvN$
under a linear cost model (tool outputs range from a line to a file).
Forcing $1$ to
$10\,\%$ of turns to miss adds the mixture, which then supplies
$\wkMixLinMin$ to $\wkMixLinMax\,\%$ of the variance under the linear
model and $\wkMixAttMin$ to $\wkMixAttMax\,\%$ once attention
dominates; the mixture rivals the appends only once eviction is active
(\S\ref{sec:sim}). What the price of a miss must capture is therefore not a ratio of
$\mathrm{CV}^2$ but the rise of $\lambda\E[S^2]/(2(1-\rho))$ that one
more miss causes.

\subsection{The Price of a Miss}\label{sec:problem}\label{sec:price}

At a decision epoch each replica holds a set of paused programs.
Program~$i$ holds $c_i$ bytes, resumes with probability~$p_i$ after an
expected further $\tau_i$ seconds, and its next turn needs prefill
$S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}$ if the state is resident on the replica that
serves it and $S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}=S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}+\Delta S_i$ if it
must be recomputed; with a shared store a remote state costs a transfer
instead. The controller keeps, offloads or drops each state, places
each resuming turn, and admits sessions, subject to resident bytes at
most~$M$ per replica, to minimise $L=L_P+L_D$ of Table~\ref{tab:model},
which by Little's law is the delay accumulated per unit time.
Relaxing the memory constraint with a multiplier $\theta\ge0$, the
shadow price of a byte-second, gives an additive cost: keeping
program~$i$ costs $\theta c_i\tau_i$, and dropping it costs the price
of the miss it may cause. A single eviction is a transient event. In the stationary model we
price it as a small change of policy: a fraction $q_i$ of arrivals,
those of program~$i$, turn from hits into misses, and we ask how $L_P$
responds. Applied to one eviction this is an approximation;
\S\ref{sec:sim} and \S\ref{sec:exp-testbed} test the stationary form.

\begin{proposition}[The price of a miss]\label{prop:price}
Model the prefill stage as an M/G/1 FIFO server with arrival rate
$\lambda\ge0$, load $\rho<1$ and mean wait $W=\E[W_q^P]$
from~\eqref{eq:pk}. Turn a fraction $q_i\ge0$ of arrivals from hits of
work $S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}\ge0$ into misses of work
$S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}\ge S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}$ such that the new load
$\rho'=\rho+\lambda\sum_iq_i\Delta S_i$ is below one, and let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:price}
  \Phi_i=\Delta S_i
   +\frac{\lambda\bigl((S_i^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})^2\bigr)}{2(1-\rho)}
   +\frac{\lambda W\,\Delta S_i}{1-\rho}.
\end{equation}
Then the change in $L_P$ satisfies
$\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i\le\Delta L_P\le\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho'}\,
\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i$.
\provedby{\leanref{missPrice\_lower}, \leanref{missPrice\_upper}, \leanref{missPrice\_aggregate\_exact}}
\end{proposition}

$\Phi_i$ is in seconds: the TTFT, summed over all turns, that one more
miss of program~$i$ adds; misses of program~$i$ occur at rate
$\lambda q_i$, so $\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i$ is a rise in the mean number
in the prefill stage by Little's law. Its three terms are three ways a
miss hurts. The miss itself takes longer to prefill. Turns that arrive
while it is being prefilled wait for all of it, and that wait grows
with the square of the miss less the square of the hit, as the second
moment in~\eqref{eq:pk} does; this is the externality of the economics
of queues \citep{naor1969,mendelson1990}, applied to a job whose size
changes. Its extra work raises the load, which stretches every wait by
$1/(1-\rho)$. The sum of these prices never overstates the damage and
understates it only by the factor $(1-\rho)/(1-\rho')$. Which term
dominates depends on the load: the second term is
$\lambda(S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}+S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2(1-\rho))$ times the
first and $(S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}+S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2W)$ times the
third,\provedby{\leanref{missPrice\_own\_vs\_queue\_ratio}, \leanref{missPrice\_queue\_terms\_ratio}}
so when few turns arrive during a miss the price is close to
$\Delta S_i$, when many arrive the turns queued behind it dominate, and
once the mean wait exceeds half the sum of the miss and hit work the
load term takes over and
diverges as the stage approaches capacity.\provedby{\leanref{missPrice\_unbounded}}

Within a replica, at one load and equal append, $\Phi_i$ is
nondecreasing in the context $K_i$, since $\Delta S_i=aK_i+bK_i^2/2$
and every term of \eqref{eq:price} grows with
it,\provedby{\leanref{prefillWork\_miss\_delta}, \leanref{missPrice\_mono\_context}}
so the load alone never reorders programs of equal append by $\Phi_i$.
It does reorder them by the expected price per byte $p_i\Phi_i/c_i$
once $p_i$ differs: with $K_c=50$k tokens, a $200$k-token program
resuming with probability $0.3$ and a $20$k-token one resuming with
probability $0.9$ change order between an idle queue and
$\rho=0.5$,\provedby{\leanref{price\_order\_flips\_with\_load}} which
no fixed key reproduces.

The M/G/1 model assumes an unbounded population. A pool of a few
million tokens holds about ten sessions of the workload of
\S\ref{sec:exp-traces}, and a session that is waiting for prefill
cannot submit its next turn, so fewer turns arrive while the queue is
long. For exponential work the finite-source model captures this
exactly.

\begin{proposition}[Finite population]\label{prop:finite}
Let $N\ge1$ sessions each think for a time of mean $Z>0$ and then
submit a turn of exponential work with mean $1/\mu$ to one FIFO server.
The mean number at the server with $n$ sessions is given exactly by
mean value analysis \citep[\S6]{lazowska1984} as $Q_0=0$,
$Q_{n}=n\,(1+Q_{n-1})/(\mu Z+1+Q_{n-1})$; with $N$ sessions the
utilisation is $\rho_N=N/(\mu Z+1+Q_{N-1})<1$ and the mean wait in
queue is $W_N=Q_{N-1}/\mu$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item $Q_n$ is nondecreasing in $n$, $Q_n\le n$, and $Q_n$ is
  nonincreasing in $\mu Z$.
\item $W_N\le\rho_N/(\mu(1-\rho_N))$, the open M/M/1 wait at the same
  utilisation.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{mvaQ\_mono}, \leanref{mvaQ\_le\_card}, \leanref{mvaQ\_anti\_c}, \leanref{finite\_source\_rho\_lt\_one}, \leanref{finite\_source\_wait\_le\_open}}
\end{proposition}

The think-time law enters only through $Z$, since the delay station
is insensitive. Part~(ii) says that the wait $W$ in the price of
Proposition~\ref{prop:price} is at least the Markovian finite-source
wait at the same utilisation; with two sessions and $Z=2/\mu$ the open wait is $4.5$ times
it,\provedby{\leanref{finite\_source\_two\_sessions\_example}} and
Table~\ref{tab:sim-finite} shows the ratio falling to
$\simFiniteRatioMin$ at $N=\simFiniteNMax$. Since at most $N$ turns
exist, a policy change with $N$ live sessions raises $L_P$ by at most
$N-L_P$, whatever the work law,\provedby{\leanref{closed\_price\_cap}}
and the last part of~(i) gives a finite-source price of longer work,
the rise of $Q_N$ when the mean work grows; \S\ref{sec:sim} and
\S\ref{sec:exp-testbed} compare both prices with the rise a real
workload shows.

\begin{proposition}[The decode stage prices only work]\label{prop:decode}
Consider the PS decode stage of \S\ref{sec:batch} with any nondecreasing
capacity~$\varphi$ and load $\rho_D<\sup_m\varphi(m)$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item $L_D$ is nondecreasing in $\rho_D$. Hence, at every load, a policy
  whose turns add no more expected demand than another's leaves no more
  turns in the batch.
\item For constant capacity $C$ and added demand $\lambda\sum_i q_i\Delta D_i$
  raising the load to $\rho_D'<C$, the change in $L_D$ is exactly
  $\frac{C-\rho_D}{C-\rho_D'}\,\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi^D_i$ with
  $\Phi^D_i=C\,\Delta D_i/(C-\rho_D)^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{stationaryMean\_mono}, \leanref{ps\_eviction\_rank\_by\_work}, \leanref{psNum\_diff\_exact}, \leanref{psPrice\_lower}, \leanref{psPrice\_upper}, \leanref{psPrice\_unbounded}}
\end{proposition}

A miss adds no decode demand, so to first order the decode stage is
untouched by it; the load factor of part~(ii) is what a decision that
adds decode demand, such as admitting a session, pays. The prefill
queue is where a miss is paid for, with a head-of-line term that
batching does not remove, and the decode batch is where the memory a
kept state occupies would otherwise buy capacity. We define the
expected price of dropping program~$i$'s state, its price per byte and
its price per byte-second held as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility}
  w_i = p_i\,\Phi_i, \qquad v_i=\frac{w_i}{c_i}, \qquad
  u_i=\frac{w_i}{c_i\,\tau_i}.
\end{equation}

\section{Congestion-Priced Scheduling}\label{sec:sched}
% ===========================================================================

The formulation leaves the scheduler two numbers. Each paused program
has a price per byte-second $u_i$ from~\eqref{eq:utility}, and each
replica has a shadow price $\theta$ for a byte-second of its memory.
Every KV decision compares the two. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cps} states the
scheduler; this section derives its steps.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Congestion-priced scheduling at one replica}
\label{alg:cps}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Observe} the prefill arrival rate $\lambda$, load $\rho$
  and mean wait $W$, and the memory $\Delta C$ that must be freed.
\STATE \textbf{Price} each paused program: $\Phi_i$
  from~\eqref{eq:price} with $\Delta S_i$ from~\eqref{eq:prefill},
  $w_i=p_i\Phi_i$, $u_i=w_i/(c_i\tau_i)$.
\STATE \textbf{Set} $\theta$ to the smallest value at which the blocks
  of the programs with $u_i\le\theta$ free $\Delta C$; $\theta=0$ if
  nothing must be freed.
\STATE \textbf{Evict} the blocks of the programs with $u_i\le\theta$,
  or whole programs by the guarded greedy (\S\ref{sec:evict}).
\STATE \textbf{Keep, offload or drop} each remaining state by the least
  of $\theta c_i\tau_i$, the priced fetch and $w_i$
  (\S\ref{sec:admission}).
\STATE \textbf{Place} a resuming turn at the replica~$j$ minimising
  $W_j+S_j+R_j+F_j$ (wait, service, move cost, future cost at~$j$,
  \S\ref{sec:routing}), with $R_j=\Phi_i$ at replica~$j$'s load, or the
  priced fetch if the state is in a shared store.
\STATE \textbf{Admit} sessions up to the count at which the expected
  resident KV of \S\ref{sec:admission} fills the pool; hold the rest in
  an entry queue.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The inputs are estimates: $\Delta S_i$ from the cost
model~\eqref{eq:prefill} calibrated once per replica
(\S\ref{sec:exp-testbed}), $p_i$ and $\tau_i$ from the program's phase,
tool and gap so far (\S\ref{sec:evict}), and the prefill queue's rate,
load and wait as running averages. The shadow price is the $u_i$ of the
marginal resident block, zero while the pool has room. Prices change
with load, so a program's rank can change while it
waits,\provedby{\leanref{price\_order\_flips\_with\_load}} which is the
point of pricing at decision time.

\subsection{Eviction}\label{sec:evict}

When memory runs out the controller must free $\Delta C$ bytes at the
least price,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:evict-priced}
  \min_{S}\ \sum_{i\in S}w_i \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{i\in S}c_i\ge\Delta C ,
\end{equation}
a covering knapsack, NP-hard in general and approximable within a
constant factor \citep{csirik1991,carnes2008}. Engines evict blocks
rather than programs, but from the tail of a context, and by
\eqref{eq:prefill} recomputing the last $m$ tokens of a context of $K$
costs $am+bm(K-m/2)$, concave in $m$: a context's deeper blocks cost
less per byte than its tail, and freeing tokens from one context costs
no more than freeing as many from two.\provedby{\leanref{tailRecompute\_marginal\_antitone}, \leanref{tailRecompute\_subadditive}}
The block problem is therefore no fractional knapsack, and choosing
programs remains its core. Its fractional relaxation, in which a program
can be given up in part at its average price per byte, is solved by
price per byte \citep{dantzig1957}: for any $\theta\ge0$ the states with
$v_i\le\theta$ cost no more than any other set that frees as much
memory, and taking programs in increasing $v_i$ until the target is
reached costs at most any feasible set plus one
program.\provedby{\leanref{threshold\_rule\_optimal}, \leanref{density\_prefix\_plus\_one}}
The same holds per byte-second, with $c_i\tau_i$ in place of $c_i$
and a target in byte-seconds,\provedby{\leanref{threshold\_rule\_optimal\_byte\_seconds}}
which is the order Algorithm~\ref{alg:cps} uses; its target is the
bytes to free now, so ordering by $u_i$ against a byte target is a
heuristic mapping of that rule, and the simulation tests it
(\S\ref{sec:sim}). The price needs the end of a program: a finished
program resumes with $p_i=0$, but an engine that keeps its blocks, as
vLLM's does, prices them as those of a paused one (\S\ref{sec:sim}). Where programs must be evicted whole,
price-per-byte order can fail by any factor; the standard repair, a
greedy guarded by a guess of the dearest evicted program, costs at most
twice the optimum \citep{csirik1991} for any prices $w_i$.\provedby{\leanref{densityFirst\_unbounded}, \leanref{threshold\_prefix\_le}, \leanref{guardedGreedy\_two\_approx}}

\paragraph{Fixed keys.} Deployed systems evict by a key fixed in
advance, the shortest context first \citep{thunderagent}, the least
recently used, the longest idle or the expired time-to-live
\citep{li2025continuum,xia2026mori}: Algorithm~\ref{alg:cps} with
$u_i$ replaced by a quantity that does not depend on $p_i$.

\begin{proposition}[Price-blind keys]\label{prop:blind}
Consider~\eqref{eq:evict-priced} with $w_i=p_i\Phi_i$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item Let a rule choose which of two programs to evict from any
  information except their resume probabilities. For every $R$ there
  are resume probabilities in $(0,1]$ under which the program it evicts
  costs more than $R$ times the other, while either program alone frees
  the target. No such rule has a constant approximation ratio
  for~\eqref{eq:evict-priced}.
\item If every program resumes and its price is its recompute work
  alone, so that $w_i\propto c_i^2$ by~\eqref{eq:prefill}, then shortest-first
  is the price-per-byte order: it costs at most twice any feasible set,
  and the factor~2 is tight.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{price\_blind\_rule\_unbounded}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_feasible}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_two\_approx}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_two\_approx\_tight}}
\end{proposition}

The proposition takes programs as the units of eviction. Part~(i) holds
as well when an engine evicts blocks: its two candidates can be the tail
blocks of two programs that differ only in $p_i$, since the argument
needs nothing but a common positive recompute
cost.\provedby{\leanref{price\_blind\_rule\_unbounded\_cost}} Part~(ii)
is about whole programs.

Part~(ii) is the regime in which shortest-first is defensible: light
prefill load, where the price is close to $\Delta S_i$, and programs
that all resume. Even there it is not optimal: with contexts $4,5,6$
and a target of $6$ it evicts $\{4,5\}$ at cost~41 while $\{6\}$ is
feasible at cost~36,\provedby{\leanref{shortestFirst\_not\_optimal}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_optimality\_claim\_false}}
contrary to the optimality theorem of
\citet[Def.~4.1, App.~F.3]{thunderagent}. Outside that regime part~(i)
applies to every key that ignores $p_i$. How much $p_i$ and $\tau_i$
vary is a workload question (Table~\ref{tab:resume},
Appendix~\ref{app:traces}): in the production traces the probability
of another turn within ten minutes is \wkPmin{} to \wkPmax{} however
one conditions, while the expected gap varies from \wkTauMin{} to
\wkTauMax{}\,s. There the byte-second denominator of $u_i$, not $p_i$,
separates programs, so a time-to-live key is closer to the price than
shortest-first; part~(i) says what a key cannot see, not how often it
is fooled.

\subsection{Placement}\label{sec:routing}

A resuming turn can be served where its state is or elsewhere. The
router charges each replica~$j$ the wait and service there, the cost
$R_j$ of moving or recomputing the state, and the expected future cost
$F_j$ of the program's remaining turns. With replica~1 holding the
state ($R_1=0$), the rule $\min_j W_j+S_j+R_j+F_j$ keeps the turn
there iff
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lookahead}
  (W_1+S_1)-(W_2+S_2) < R_2+(F_2-F_1).
\end{equation}
\provedby{\leanref{lookahead\_prefers\_iff}}
The price of a miss supplies $R_j$: a move turns the next turn into a
miss served at replica~$j$, so $R_j$ is $\Phi_i$ at that replica's
prefill load, small at an idle replica even for a long context.
Session affinity, the rule that never moves a program, sets
$R_j=\infty$ elsewhere. Against an idle replica with the same service
rate $\mu$ for whole turns, moving costs $1/\mu+R+F$, and the M/M/1
response time of
the affinity replica exceeds it exactly when its utilisation exceeds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhostar}
  \rho^*=\frac{\mu(R+F)}{1+\mu(R+F)},
\end{equation}
which is nondecreasing in $R$ and
$F$.\provedby{\leanref{affinity\_loses\_iff}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_stable}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_utilization}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_mono}, \leanref{affinity\_not\_always\_optimal}, \leanref{mm1Wait\_unbounded}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_examples}}
The idle alternative gives the earliest inversion, so $\rho^*$ is a
lower bound on the load at which affinity loses to any real
alternative. With a shared KV store $R_j$ becomes the priced fetch of
\S\ref{sec:admission} and that load falls: affinity and a store are
alternatives rather than complements.

\subsection{Admission and Offloading}\label{sec:admission}

The pool holds both the batch's working set and the paused states,
and the two feed each other: a miss stays longer and re-inserts its
context, which evicts paused state and causes more misses. The loop
amplifies misses at moderate load (\S\ref{sec:exp-testbed}) and
thrashes the replica at high load (\S\ref{sec:sim}), as
\citet{ao2026congestion} find without reuse. Admission keeps the pool
out of that regime. Let a session live for a time $T_s$ and hold $K(t)$ tokens at
age~$t$. Treating live sessions as an M/G/$\infty$ system (each session
its own server, none queues), Campbell's theorem \citep{kingman1993}
gives the mean resident KV as $\Lambda\,\E[\int_0^{T_s}K(t)\,dt]$,
which exceeds the naive $\Lambda\,\E[T_s]\,\E[K]$ whenever
long contexts belong to long sessions (by $\wkKvRatio\times$ in the
production traces and $\trKvRatio\times$ in our SWE-bench Pro runs).
The admission step caps live sessions where this estimate fills the
pool; \S\ref{sec:sim} shows that the cap, not the eviction key, decides
whether the replica thrashes. The cap has a price of its own, the wait at the entry queue, and a
session is worth admitting while the memory it will hold, at $\theta$
per byte-second, costs less than that wait.

A state is kept, offloaded to a slower tier or dropped by the least of
$\theta c_i\tau_i$, the priced fetch and $w_i$, where the priced fetch is $\Phi_i$ with the
transfer time in place of $\Delta S_i$ when the fetch holds the prefill
stage, plus the tier's wait once it saturates. Since offloading only
enlarges the choice set, enabling it never raises the optimal cost,
while always offloading can be worse than never
offloading,\provedby{\leanref{optimal\_cost\_antitone\_in\_actions}, \leanref{enabling\_offload\_never\_hurts}, \leanref{always\_offload\_can\_be\_worse}}
so the collapse of an always-offloading stack reported by
\citet[App.~A.2]{thunderagent} is a property of that policy, not of
the mechanism. Every rule in use replaces one price by a
constant: shortest-first sets $w_i\propto c_i^2$, recency and time-to-live
keys set $p_i$ to a constant, always offloading sets the priced fetch
to zero, strict affinity sets $R_j=\infty$, and admitting every session
sets $\theta=0$.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

Charging a job for the delay it imposes on others is classical in the
economics of queues \citep{naor1969,mendelson1990}; the size-squared
term of~\eqref{eq:price} is the PK second moment of that externality.
We claim the application (a job whose size changes with a cache
decision), the two-stage separation, the finite-population bound and
the one price across four decisions, not the formula. LLM queueing
analyses take the variability from output length
\citep{mitzenmacher2025queueing,yang2024mg1,ozbas2026reasoning,shahout2024trail},
and \citet{ao2026congestion} show the equilibrium of a
memory-constrained replica unstable; none models KV reuse across the turns of a session, so
none prices a miss; online KV scheduling has competitive policies and
hardness results
\citep{jaillet2025online,feng2026nonclairvoyant,wang2025variable,dexter2025prefix}.

Among systems that keep the KV of paused programs
\citep{thunderagent,infercept}, Continuum \citep{li2025continuum} sets
a time-to-live from the reload cost and the queueing delay an eviction
would induce, our price without its head-of-line term and load factor;
MORI \citep{xia2026mori} offloads by idleness; others predict reuse or
trade a retained prefix against batch memory
\citep{bian2025tokencake,pan2025kvflow,zhang2026cachescout,ni2026topas};
KVLearn \citep{kvlearn2026} learns retention from the inputs of $u_i$
and $\theta$. Chunked prefill \citep{sarathi2024} leaves
head-of-line blocking among prefills
\citep{hsieh2026flowprefill,liu2026chunkedfair,lyu2025fairbatching},
the term our price keeps.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
% ===========================================================================

The propositions are about the model, not the world, and of the
measurements of Appendix~\ref{app:design} only the cost fit and the
two replays have been run
(one stack, one derived workload, one forced share, four ranks, two
seeds). \eqref{eq:pk} is an approximation: session
feedback makes arrivals non-Poisson, and on our stack a prefill step is
exclusive and the ranks step in lockstep, so decode gets what prefill
leaves. The propositions concern means and a stationary fraction of
misses, not tails, one eviction or block waits.
It is falsified by a prefill wait off \eqref{eq:pk} with the pool
slack, or a blind key beating the price.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work provides analytical tools for capacity planning and scheduling
in LLM serving systems. Better utilisation of serving hardware lowers the
energy and cost of deploying language models; we see no direct negative
societal consequences beyond those generally associated with more
efficient LLM deployment.

\sloppy
Every proposition and every derived number in the paper has also been
formalised and machine-checked in Lean~4 with Mathlib; the development
is in the supplementary material. The proofs below are written for
human readers and do not follow the formal proofs step by step. The
known results quoted in \S\ref{sec:evict} (the threshold rule, the
block prefix and the guarded greedy) and the closed
form~\eqref{eq:rhostar} are verified there as well and not re-proved
here. For Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} the mean value recursion is
taken as the model, as the arrival theorem gives it; the inequalities
are proved from it.


\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:decode}]
(i) Write $a_m=1/\prod_{k\le m}\varphi(k)$, so
$L(\rho)=\sum_mma_m\rho^m/\sum_ma_m\rho^m$. For $\rho_1\le\rho_2$,
\[
  L(\rho_2)-L(\rho_1)=\frac{\sum_{m,j}ma_ma_j(\rho_2^m\rho_1^j-\rho_1^m\rho_2^j)}{P(\rho_1)P(\rho_2)},
\]
with $P(\rho)=\sum_ma_m\rho^m$. Exchanging $m$ and $j$ and averaging, the
numerator is half of
$\sum_{m,j}a_ma_j(m-j)(\rho_2^m\rho_1^j-\rho_1^m\rho_2^j)$. For $m\ge j$
the last factor is $\rho_1^j\rho_2^j(\rho_2^{m-j}-\rho_1^{m-j})\ge0$, and
symmetrically for $m<j$ both factors are non-positive, so every term is
non-negative. This holds for every truncation of the sums at a finite
$M$, and $L$ is the limit of the truncated means, so it holds for $L$.
By insensitivity, $L$ depends on the policy only through $\rho$, and a
policy that adds expected demand $A$ per arrival moves the load to
$\rho+\lambda A$.

(ii) With constant capacity $\pi$ is geometric and $L=\rho/(C-\rho)$.
With $\rho'=\rho+\lambda A$ and $A=\sum_iq_i\Delta D_i$,
\[
  L(\rho')-L(\rho)=\frac{C\,\lambda A}{(C-\rho)(C-\rho')}
  =\frac{C-\rho}{C-\rho'}\cdot\frac{C\,\lambda A}{(C-\rho)^2},
\]
and the last factor is $\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi^D_i$. Since $\rho'\ge\rho$ the
prefactor is at least one.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:finite}]
Write $c=\mu Z$ and $g(q)=(1+q)/(c+1+q)$, so $Q_n=n\,g(Q_{n-1})$. The
recursion is mean value analysis for the closed network of one FIFO
server and one delay station: by the arrival theorem a turn arriving
with $n$ sessions in the system sees the stationary state with $n-1$,
so its response time is $R_n=(1+Q_{n-1})/\mu$, the throughput is
$X_n=n/(Z+R_n)$ and $Q_n=X_nR_n$. Dividing, $\rho_n=X_n/\mu=
n/(c+1+Q_{n-1})$ and $Q_n=\rho_n(1+Q_{n-1})$.

(i) $g$ is nonnegative and nondecreasing on $q\ge0$, and
$g\le1$. Hence $0\le Q_n\le n$ by induction, and
$Q_{n+1}=(n+1)g(Q_n)\ge n\,g(Q_{n-1})=Q_n$ once $Q_n\ge Q_{n-1}$,
which holds at $n=1$.

$\rho_n<1$: it is equivalent to $Q_{n-1}>n-1-c$, and $Q_n>n-c$ holds
for all $n$ by induction, since
$Q_{n+1}-(n+1-c)=c\,(Q_n+c-n)/(c+1+Q_n)>0$.

$Q_n$ is nonincreasing in $c$: $g$ is nonincreasing in $c$ at fixed
$q$, so if $Q_{n-1}(c')\le Q_{n-1}(c)$ for $c\le c'$ then
$Q_n(c')=n\,g_{c'}(Q_{n-1}(c'))\le n\,g_{c'}(Q_{n-1}(c))\le
n\,g_c(Q_{n-1}(c))=Q_n(c)$.

(ii) $W_N\le\rho_N/(\mu(1-\rho_N))$ is $Q_{N-1}(1-\rho_N)\le\rho_N$,
that is $Q_{N-1}\le\rho_N(1+Q_{N-1})=Q_N$, which is~(i).

The cap stated after the proposition: the mean number at the server of
a closed system of $N$ sessions lies in $[0,N]$ under any policy, so
$L_P'-L_P\le N-L_P$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:price} and of the remarks after it]
Write $u=1-\rho$, $m_2=\E[S^2]$,
$A=\sum_iq_i\Delta S_i$ and
$B=\sum_iq_i\bigl((S_i^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})^2\bigr)$.
The change raises $\E[S]$ by $A$ and $\E[S^2]$ by $B$, so the load
becomes $\rho'=\rho+\lambda A$ and $1-\rho'=u-\lambda A>0$. By
\eqref{eq:pk}, $L=\lambda^2m_2/(2u)+\rho$, hence
\[
  \Delta L=\lambda A+\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\Bigl(\frac{m_2+B}{u-\lambda A}
    -\frac{m_2}{u}\Bigr)
  =\lambda A+\frac{Y}{u(u-\lambda A)},
\]
with $Y=\lambda^2(uB+\lambda Am_2)/2\ge0$. Summing \eqref{eq:price} with
$W=\lambda m_2/(2u)$ gives $\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i=\lambda A+Y/u^2$.

For the bracket, since $0<u-\lambda A\le u$, $Y/(u(u-\lambda A))\ge Y/u^2$, which is
the lower bound. For the upper bound,
\[
  \frac{u}{u-\lambda A}\Bigl(\lambda A+\frac{Y}{u^2}\Bigr)-\Delta L
  =\frac{(\lambda A)^2}{u-\lambda A}\ \ge\ 0 .
\]

For the ratios stated after the proposition, $(S^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S^{\mathrm{hit}})^2
=(S^{\mathrm{miss}}+S^{\mathrm{hit}})\Delta S$, so the second term of
$\Phi_i$ is $\lambda(S^{\mathrm{miss}}+S^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2u)$ times
$\Delta S_i$, and the third is $\lambda W\Delta S_i/u$. Dividing gives
both ratios.

For the divergence, let $m_1=\E[S]$ and $\lambda=(1-\varepsilon)/m_1$ with
$0<\varepsilon\le1/2$, so $\rho=1-\varepsilon$ and $\lambda\ge1/(2m_1)$.
The first two terms of $\Phi_i$ are non-negative, and the third is
$\lambda^2m_2\Delta S_i/(2\varepsilon^2)\ge m_2\Delta S_i/(8m_1^2\varepsilon^2)$,
which exceeds any bound for small~$\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}]
(i) Take two programs of the same size $c>0$ that agree in everything
the rule observes, and a target $\Delta C=c$, so that either program
alone is feasible. The rule sees the same input whatever the resume
probabilities are, so it evicts a fixed one of the two, say $e$, and let
$o$ be the other. Set $p_e=1$ and $p_o=1/(|R|+2)$, both in $(0,1]$. The
evicted program costs $p_ec^2=c^2$, while $o$ costs
$c^2/(|R|+2)$, and $R\cdot c^2/(|R|+2)<c^2$ since $R<|R|+2$. The
optimum is at most the cost of $o$, so the rule's ratio exceeds~$R$.

(ii) By~\eqref{eq:prefill} the recompute work of a context of $c$
tokens is $ac+bc^2/2$; the proposition takes the quadratic term as the
price, $w_i=c_i^2$, so $w_i/c_i=c_i$ and price-per-byte order is
shortest-first (SF). SF frees $\Delta C$ whenever the pool holds it: it
stops only at a program at least as long as the remaining target or
when the pool is exhausted. For the ratio, let SF take
$t_1\le\dots\le t_m$, the first $m$ programs in sorted order. Write $\ell=t_m$ and $\sigma=t_1+\dots+t_{m-1}$, so $\sigma<\Delta C$ (SF did
not stop earlier). Let $S$ be any feasible set.

\emph{Step 1: $\ell^2\le\mathrm{cost}(S)$.} Every program with $c_i<\ell$ is
among the first $m-1$ in sorted order, and these free only $\sigma<\Delta C$.
So $S$ contains a program with $c_i\ge \ell$, whose cost alone is at
least~$\ell^2$.

\emph{Step 2: a lower bound on $\mathrm{cost}(S)$.} Let
$f(x)=x(\ell-x)$ for $x<\ell$ and $f(x)=0$ otherwise, so $f\ge0$ on $[0,\infty)$
and $x^2\ge \ell x-f(x)$ for all $x\ge0$ (with equality for $x<\ell$, and
$x^2\ge \ell x$ for $x\ge \ell$). Summing over $S$ and using
$\sum_{i\in S}c_i\ge\Delta C$,
\[
  \mathrm{cost}(S)\ \ge\ \ell\,\Delta C-\sum_{i\in S}f(c_i)
  \ \ge\ \ell\,\Delta C-\sum_{j<m}t_j(\ell-t_j),
\]
because only programs with $c_i<\ell$ contribute to the sum, all of them
are among $t_1,\dots,t_{m-1}$, and $t_j(\ell-t_j)\ge0$ for $j<m$. The right
side equals $\ell(\Delta C-\sigma)+\sum_{j<m}t_j^2$.

\emph{Step 3.} Since $\Delta C-\sigma>0$,
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{cost}(\mathrm{SF})&=\textstyle\sum_{j<m}t_j^2+\ell^2\\
  &\le \mathrm{cost}(S)-\ell(\Delta C-\sigma)+\ell^2\\
  &\le \mathrm{cost}(S)+\ell^2,
\end{align*}
and Step~1 bounds the last term by $\mathrm{cost}(S)$.

Tightness: on $K,K+1$ with $\Delta C=K+1$, SF takes $K$ (leaving $1$), then
$K+1$, at cost $K^2+(K+1)^2$, while $\{K+1\}$ costs $(K+1)^2$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Block eviction (\S\ref{sec:evict}).} The proof of~(i) uses
only that the two candidates cost the same positive amount before the
resume probabilities weigh them, so it applies unchanged to the tail
blocks of two programs that agree in everything but $p_i$. For the tail
of one context, let $f(m)=am+bm(K-m/2)$ be the recompute of its last $m$
tokens. Then $f(m+d)-f(m)=ad+bd(K-m-d/2)$ is nonincreasing in $m$ for
$b,d\ge0$, so deeper blocks cost no more per byte, and
$f(m_1+m_2)-f(m_1)-f(m_2)=-bm_1m_2\le0$.

\section{Experimental Design for a Real System}\label{app:design}\label{sec:exp-real}\label{sec:exp-setup}\label{sec:exp-protocol}\label{sec:exp-calib}\label{sec:exp-variance}\label{sec:exp-offload}\label{sec:exp-evict}\label{sec:exp-routing}\label{sec:exp-scorecard}

Table~\ref{tab:exp-overview} lists the experiments that will decide
whether the propositions carry over to a rack-scale NPU testbed running
a serving stack with prefix caching, KV offloading and a pluggable
router, on replayed coding-agent traces and a synthetic chat workload.
Decision accuracy is the primary metric: whether the model picks the
same policy as the real optimum and ranks policies in the same order
(Kendall~$\tau$ over policy sets), reported alongside the error of
TTFT, TPOT and throughput on held-out workloads. The \emph{cost fit}
fits \eqref{eq:prefill}, \eqref{eq:decode} and $\varphi(m)$ and
measures the batch that fits under the trace's footprint law. The
\emph{replays} test whether the prefill wait tracks
$\lambda\E[S^2]/(2(1-\rho))$, report the number of live sessions next
to the load, and force misses on a controlled fraction of turns to
compare the rise in summed TTFT with Proposition~\ref{prop:price}. The
\emph{offloading test} asks whether the priced keep/offload/drop choice
is never below never-offload while always-offload falls below it once
the tier saturates. The \emph{eviction replay} replays eviction events:
shortest-first within a factor~2 of the optimum when resume
probabilities are uniform, the byte-second key ahead when pause
times differ, and the admission cap swept end to end, since the
simulation says it decides whether eviction matters at all. The
\emph{placement test} measures the move cost and the load at which
affinity loses, against $\rho^*$ of~\eqref{eq:rhostar}. The
\emph{faithfulness} scoring scores the model and the calibrated
simulator against the testbed. The result-table layouts are in the
research plan distributed with the code; the cost fit and the replays
are reported in \S\ref{sec:exp-testbed} and Appendix~\ref{app:exp}.
