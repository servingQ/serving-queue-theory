\begin{abstract}
An agentic LLM server keeps the KV cache of suspended programs across
tool calls, and every KV decision asks what losing one of these states
costs. We model sessions looping through turns and tool calls on a replica
with a prefill queue, a decode batch and one memory pool, and derive the
\emph{price of a miss}: the total time to first token it adds across
all turns. Beyond its own recompute time, a miss
holds up every prefill queued behind it, a term quadratic in its length
that chunked prefill does not remove, and it raises the load that
stretches every wait. The price is exact to first order and unbounded
near capacity, so no fixed rule, shortest-context-first included, ranks
states correctly at every load. We propose congestion-priced
scheduling, where one shadow price of memory decides eviction,
offloading and admission. An uncalibrated simulation checks the
propositions and finds that memory thrashing, not eviction order,
decides throughput.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Response time is queueing delay plus service time, $T=W_q+S$.
Hardware benchmarks measure~$S$; users experience~$T$. Near saturation
$W_q$ dominates and grows without bound: in the simplest Markovian
model, raising the load from $\rho=0.90$ to $0.99$ multiplies response
time by ten.\provedby{\leanref{mm1Wait\_example\_ratio}} The serving
question is therefore not how many tokens per second a device delivers
but which control knob moves the point where TTFT crosses its SLA.

Agentic workloads sharpen the problem. In production coding-agent traces
the median session has 43 LLM turns, a median input of 142K tokens, a
median output of 444 tokens and a prefix-cache hit rate above 96\,\%
\citep{vllm-agentx}. Each turn appends a few hundred tokens to a context of
$10^5$ tokens, interleaved with tool calls of unpredictable duration. The
unit of work that matters is the \emph{program} (a multi-turn session with
persistent KV state), not the request \citep{thunderagent}. While a
program waits for a tool, its KV cache occupies device memory without
doing work.

Every KV decision in such a system asks one question: what does it cost
to lose a suspended program's state? The obvious answer is the time to
recompute it. In a queue that answer is incomplete. A miss is long work
added to a shared server, and every other request pays for it. How much
they pay depends on how loaded the system already is, and on whether the
miss shares the batch or blocks it. Near saturation the payment is what
matters, and it is where fixed heuristics are least reliable.

Queueing models of LLM serving have so far asked when a server is
stable and which schedulers are throughput-optimal, with output length
as the source of variability
\citep{yang2024mg1,dai2025workconserving,ao2025fluid,nie2026stability}.
Agentic serving systems, for their part, keep or evict the KV of
suspended programs by rules of thumb such as recency, time-to-live or
idleness \citep{li2025continuum,xia2026mori,thunderagent}. We connect
the two: the variability that matters here is in the prefill work, it
has two sources, the size of the append and the hit/miss mixture that
the KV policy creates, and the second, the only one the scheduler
controls, can be priced per miss and used as the policy's decision
variable.

This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A formulation and its central result} (\S\ref{sec:model}).
Sessions arrive as a Poisson process and loop through turns and tools. A
replica has two resources, a prefill queue that serves pending prefills
in order and a decode batch that shares bandwidth, and one memory pool
that the batch and the suspended states share. A miss changes only the
prefill work, and we derive its price at the prefill queue
(Proposition~\ref{prop:price}); the decode stage prices only work and is
insensitive to it (Proposition~\ref{prop:decode}).
\item \textbf{Congestion-priced scheduling} (\S\ref{sec:sched}). One
number per suspended program, its expected price per byte-second of
memory, is compared with one shadow price of memory to decide eviction,
offloading and admission; routing uses the same price, and the load at
which session affinity starts to lose has a closed form in the cost of
moving a state, which a shared KV store lowers
(Proposition~\ref{prop:routing}). The threshold
rule is optimal for the relaxed problem and optimal up to one block
when state is evicted in blocks (Proposition~\ref{prop:memory}), and
the program-level eviction rule is a 2-approximation for any prices
(Proposition~\ref{prop:guarded}). The keys in use, shortest-first,
recency and time-to-live among them, are the special cases in which the
price is replaced by a constant, and none of them has a bounded
approximation ratio once resume probabilities vary
(Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}).
\item \textbf{Experiments} (\S\ref{sec:exp}). An uncalibrated simulation
checks each proposition inside and beyond its model and shows that
memory thrashing, not the eviction order, decides throughput. An
experimental design on a rack-scale NPU testbed takes decision
faithfulness as its primary metric; no measurement is reported in this
draft.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Conventions.} Propositions are our results; their proofs are
in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Standard results are cited and not
re-proved. When we discuss another paper we report what it states, with
section and figure, and we have not re-run its experiments.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:model}
% ===========================================================================

served by one of $J$ replicas, each a prefill queue, a decode batch and

hit, and so what the prefill queue has to serve
(\S\ref{sec:congestion}). \S\ref{sec:problem} states the decision
problem and \S\ref{sec:price} derives the one quantity every decision in
it needs.

\subsection{Sessions, Turns and Tools}\label{sec:sessions}\label{sec:closed}

An agent program, or session, cycles through
$\text{prefill}\to\text{decode}\to\text{tool}\to\text{prefill}\to\cdots$.
Sessions arrive in a Poisson process of rate~$\Lambda$; inside a
session the loop is closed: a turn is served, its tool call takes a
random time, and with probability~$p_i$ the session issues another
turn. Tool time is the \emph{think time} of an interactive network
\citep{lazowska1984}. A session in a tool call is suspended future work
with resident state, and its phase and transition probabilities are
observable, which a request-level scheduler discards.

The result is an open network with feedback. Turns reach the replicas
at rate $\lambda=\Lambda\,\E[\text{turns per session}]$, correlated
within a session. For networks of processor-sharing and delay stations
the BCMP theorem gives a product-form stationary law \citep{bcmp1975}:
each station behaves, in distribution, as if fed by Poisson arrivals at
its own rate, whatever the tool-time law, and turn classes let the work
grow with the context. We use this for the decode stage and as an
approximation for the prefill stage. Benchmarks that fix the number of
concurrent programs~$N$ \citep{thunderagent} are the closed special
case; closed product-form throughput does not fall with~$N$ at fixed
demand \citep{lazowska1984}, so a throughput that does fall signals a
demand that grows, here through misses.

Each live session holds KV of size $K(t)$ that grows with its age~$t$.
Treating live sessions as an M/G/$\infty$ system, Campbell's theorem
\citep{kingman1993} gives the resident KV mean
$\Lambda\,\E[\int_0^DK(t)\,dt]$ and variance
$\Lambda\,\E[\int_0^DK(t)^2\,dt]$ over the session lifetime~$D$. The
mean exceeds the naive $\Lambda\,\E[D]\,\E[K]$ whenever long contexts
belong to long sessions (by $\wkKvRatio\times$ in the production traces
of \S\ref{sec:exp-traces}, where the two correlate at $\wkCorrDK$), and
evictions start when resident KV exceeds the pool, not when its mean
does. Longer tool calls raise memory demand without raising compute
demand. \S\ref{sec:admission} uses this estimate.

\subsection{A Replica: Two Resources and a Memory Pool}\label{sec:batch}\label{sec:queue}

A turn with context $K_i$ appends $n_i$ tokens and decodes $o_i$. It
makes three demands on its replica.
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Prefill compute.} Prefilling $n$ tokens onto a resident
  prefix of $K$ costs
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:prefill}
    P(n,K)=a\,n+b\,n\,(K+n/2),
  \end{equation}
  a linear term for the dense layers and an attention term over the
  prefix. A hit costs $P(n_i,K_i)$; a miss re-prefills the context,
  $P(K_i+n_i,0)$, quadratic in $K_i$ as ThunderAgent's Lemma~4.1 models
  it \citep{thunderagent}.
\item \emph{Decode bandwidth.} Each decode step reads the weights once
  for the batch and the KV of every turn in it, so decoding $o_i$ tokens
  moves
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:decode}
    D_i = o_i\bigl(\beta K_i+\omega/n\bigr)
  \end{equation}
  bytes-worth of time, with $n$ the batch size, $\beta$ the KV bytes per
  token and $\omega$ the weight bytes. At agentic context lengths the
  KV term dominates, and it is the same for a hit and a miss.
\item \emph{Memory.} $c_i=\beta K_i$ bytes for as long as the state is
  resident, running or suspended.
\end{itemize}
The hit/miss distinction lives entirely in the first demand.

\paragraph{Decode stage.} Continuous batching advances every turn in
the batch at each iteration, so decoding turns share the device. We
model the decode stage as a processor-sharing (PS) station with
capacity $\varphi(n)$ when $n$ turns share it, in units
of~\eqref{eq:decode}; batching makes $\varphi$ increase with $n$ and
memory makes it saturate. The stationary law of the batch size is
$\pi(n)\propto\rho_D^n/\prod_{k\le n}\varphi(k)$ with
$\rho_D=\lambda\E[D]$, and it is \emph{insensitive}: it depends on the
demand only through its mean \citep{bcmp1975,kelly1979}.

\paragraph{Prefill stage.} Chunked prefill splits a prefill into
near-equal chunks and adds them to the batch ``without pausing ongoing
decodes'' \citep{sarathi2024}: each step first serves the decode batch,
then spends the tokens left in its budget on pending prefills. Chunking
protects the decoding turns from a long prefill, but it does not let
one prefill overtake another; the schedulers we know of serve pending
prefills in arrival order. We therefore model the prefill stage as a
FIFO queue with service $P_i$ whose server is available a fraction
$1-\rho_D$ of the time. For Poisson arrivals to a FIFO server with load
$\rho<1$ and $\E[S^2]<\infty$ (M/G/1), the Pollaczek--Khinchine (PK)
formula \citep{pollaczek1930,khinchine1932} gives the mean wait
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pk}
  \E[W_q] = \frac{\lambda \E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)} ;
\end{equation}
for exponential service this is the M/M/1 response time
$1/(\mu-\lambda)$ less the service, with $\mu=1/\E[S]$
\citep{kleinrock1975}. Session feedback makes arrivals non-Poisson and
the budget fluctuates, so we use~\eqref{eq:pk} as an approximation and
check it by simulation (\S\ref{sec:sim}); Kingman's bound
\citep{kingman1962} says bursty arrivals add delay on top of service
variability over the same factor $1-\rho$. The two stages thus differ
in what they need from the cache: the decode stage needs only mean
demands, which the cache does not change; the prefill stage needs the
second moment of its work, which the cache sets.

\paragraph{Memory pool.} The batch and the suspended states share one
pool of $M$ bytes, so a replica admits turns into the batch until the
next one does not fit. The batch that fits is a property of the
footprint law and the admission rule, not of the mean footprint: with
$12$ free tokens, footprints of $6$ admit $2$ turns while footprints of
$5$ or $7$ (equally likely) admit $7/4$ on average, whereas footprints
of $7$ admit $1$ and footprints of $2$ or $12$ admit
$95/64$.\provedby{\leanref{footprint\_variance\_hurts}, \leanref{footprint\_variance\_helps}}
So $\varphi$ is calibrated (\S\ref{sec:exp-calib}), not derived. The
pool also couples the two roles of memory: every byte a suspended
session holds is a byte the batch cannot use, and every miss holds its
turn in the batch for longer. Once resident KV exceeds the pool, the
policy has to choose what to drop, and that choice sets the hit
rate~$p$. A shared KV store \citep{mooncake-vllm} adds a slower tier
that every replica can read, so a state that is not resident on the
serving replica is fetched instead of recomputed.

\subsection{Prefill Work under KV Reuse}\label{sec:congestion}

With hit probability~$p$ the prefill work of a turn is the mixture
\begin{equation*}
  S = \begin{cases} S^{\mathrm{hit}}=P(n_i,K_i) & \text{w.p. } p, \\
                    S^{\mathrm{miss}}=P(K_i+n_i,0) & \text{w.p. } 1-p, \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
so the KV policy sets $\E[S]$, $\E[S^2]$ and~$\rho_P$. At the FIFO
prefill stage, \eqref{eq:pk} makes the second moment a first-class
quantity: of two work laws with the same mean, the one with the larger
variance waits longer at every stable
load,\provedby{\leanref{pkWait\_lt\_of\_variance\_lt}} and raising the
hit rate lowers both moments at once, so the PK wait is non-increasing
in~$p$.\provedby{\leanref{meanService\_antitone}, \leanref{utilization\_antitone}, \leanref{secondMomentService\_antitone}, \leanref{pkWait\_mixture\_antitone}}

We do not assume that agentic workloads have intrinsically high
variance; we ask where it comes from. With a prefix hit rate above
96\,\% \citep{vllm-agentx} a turn's prefill is an append, and the
append itself varies: tool outputs range from a line to a file, and a
context compaction rewrites the prompt. In production traces the append
has $\mathrm{CV}^2=\wkCvN$ (\S\ref{sec:exp-traces}), so the prefill
work is variable even when every turn hits. The mixture adds a second
source that only the KV policy controls: a miss re-prefills a
$10^5$-token context and costs one to two orders of magnitude more
prefill than a hit. Relative to exponential work with the same mean and
load,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cv2}
  \frac{\E[W_q]_{M/G/1}}{\E[W_q]_{M/M/1}}=\frac{1+\mathrm{CV}^2}{2},
  \qquad \mathrm{CV}^2=\frac{\E[S^2]}{\E[S]^2}-1 .
\end{equation}
At a 96\,\% hit rate, a hit prefill of $50$\,ms and a miss of $5$\,s give
$\mathrm{CV}^2>15$, more than $8\times$ the wait of exponential work;
the same hit rate with a $100$\,ms miss gives $\mathrm{CV}^2<0.05$.
\provedby{\leanref{pkWait\_ratio\_to\_exponential}, \leanref{mixtureCV2\_agentic\_example}, \leanref{mixtureCV2\_cheap\_miss\_example}}
Both sources fall on the TTFT, which is the prefill wait, and not on the
decode stage, whose demand~\eqref{eq:decode} is the same for a hit and a
miss and whose occupancy is insensitive in any case. How the variance
splits between the append and the mixture depends on the hit rate and
on the cost model; the traces of \S\ref{sec:exp-traces} put the
mixture's share at $\wkMixShareLin\,\%$ at a $96\,\%$ hit rate under a
linear cost model, and E2 measures it (\S\ref{sec:exp-variance}).

\subsection{The KV-State Problem}\label{sec:problem}

At a decision epoch each replica holds a set of suspended programs.
Program~$i$ holds $c_i=\beta K_i$ bytes of KV, resumes with
probability~$p_i$ after an expected further $\tau_i$ seconds in its
tool call, and its next turn needs prefill $S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}$ if the
state is resident on the replica that serves it and
$S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}=S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}+\Delta S_i$ if it must be
recomputed; with a shared store a remote state costs a transfer
instead. The controller keeps, offloads or drops each state; when the
next turn arrives it chooses a replica; and it decides how many
sessions to admit. The bytes held by suspended programs and by the
batch sum to at most~$M$ on each replica. The objective is the mean
number of turns in the system,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:numsys}
  L = L_P + L_D ,
\end{equation}
waiting for prefill plus in the decode batch, which by Little's law is
the delay accumulated per unit time; $L_P$ alone is the TTFT part.
Relaxing the memory constraint with a multiplier $\theta\ge0$, the
shadow price of a byte-second of memory, gives an additive cost:
keeping program~$i$ costs $\theta\,c_i\,\tau_i$, and dropping it costs
the price of the miss it may cause.

\subsection{The Price of a Miss}\label{sec:price}

Dropping a program's state does not change the arrival rate. It changes
the prefill work of the program's next turn. In the stationary model a
policy fixes which fraction of arrivals are misses, so we ask what
happens to $L_P$ when a fraction $q_i$ of arrivals, belonging to
program~$i$, turn from hits into misses. The added prefill load is
$\rho'-\rho=\lambda\sum_iq_i\Delta S_i$.

\begin{proposition}[The price of a miss]\label{prop:price}
Model the prefill stage as an M/G/1 FIFO server with arrival rate
$\lambda\ge0$, load $\rho<1$ and mean wait $W=\E[W_q]$
from~\eqref{eq:pk}. Turn a fraction $q_i\ge0$ of arrivals from hits of
work $S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}\ge0$ into misses of work
$S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}\ge S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}$, and let
$\rho'=\rho+\lambda\sum_iq_i\Delta S_i<1$. Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:price}
  \Phi_i=\Delta S_i
   +\frac{\lambda\bigl((S_i^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})^2\bigr)}{2(1-\rho)}
   +\frac{\lambda W\,\Delta S_i}{1-\rho}.
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item The change in $L_P$ satisfies
  $\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i\le\Delta L_P\le\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho'}\,
  \lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i$.
\item The second term of $\Phi_i$ is
  $\lambda(S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}+S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2(1-\rho))$ times the
  first, and, if $W>0$,
  $(S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}+S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2W)$ times the third.
\item For fixed work with $\E[S]>0$, $\E[S^2]>0$ and
  $S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}<S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}$, $\Phi_i$ is unbounded as
  $\lambda$ approaches $1/\E[S]$.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{missPrice\_lower}, \leanref{missPrice\_upper}, \leanref{missPrice\_aggregate\_exact}, \leanref{missPrice\_own\_vs\_queue\_ratio}, \leanref{missPrice\_queue\_terms\_ratio}, \leanref{missPrice\_unbounded}}
\end{proposition}

$\Phi_i$ is the total time to first token, summed over all turns, that
one miss of program~$i$ adds, and its three terms are three ways a miss
hurts. The miss itself takes longer to prefill. Turns that arrive while
it is being prefilled wait for all of it, and that wait grows with the
square of the miss, as the second moment in~\eqref{eq:pk} does. Its
extra work raises the prefill load, which stretches every wait already
in the queue by $1/(1-\rho)$. Part~(i) says that the sum of these
prices never overstates the damage and understates it only by the
factor $(1-\rho)/(1-\rho')$, close to one when the change in load is
small. Part~(ii) says which term dominates, and the answer depends on
the load: when few turns arrive during a miss the price is close to
$\Delta S_i$; when many arrive, the turns queued behind it dominate and
the price grows with the square of the miss; once the mean wait exceeds
half the miss, the load term takes over, with a factor that diverges as
the stage approaches capacity, part~(iii). A rule fixed in advance,
whether context length, recompute time or hit ratio, ranks states the
way $\Phi$ does only in some load regime.

\begin{proposition}[The decode stage prices only work]\label{prop:decode}
Consider the PS decode stage of \S\ref{sec:batch} with any nondecreasing
capacity~$\varphi$ and load $\rho_D$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item $L_D$ is nondecreasing in $\rho_D$. Hence, at every load, a policy
  whose turns add no more expected demand than another's leaves no more
  turns in the batch.
\item For constant capacity $C$ and added demand raising the load to
  $\rho_D'<C$, the change in $L_D$ is exactly
  $\frac{C-\rho_D}{C-\rho_D'}\,\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi^D_i$ with
  $\Phi^D_i=C\,\Delta D_i/(C-\rho_D)^2$, which is unbounded as
  $\rho_D$ approaches $C$.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{stationaryMean\_mono}, \leanref{ps\_eviction\_rank\_by\_work}, \leanref{psNum\_diff\_exact}, \leanref{psPrice\_lower}, \leanref{psPrice\_upper}, \leanref{psPrice\_unbounded}}
\end{proposition}

A miss adds no decode demand, so to first order the decode stage is
untouched by it; the load factor of part~(ii) is what a decision that
adds decode demand, such as admitting a session, pays. The prefill
queue is where a miss is paid for, with a head-of-line term that
batching does not remove, and the decode batch is where the memory a
kept state occupies would otherwise buy capacity. We define the
expected price of dropping program~$i$'s state, its price per byte and
its price per byte-second held as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility}
  w_i = p_i\,\Phi_i, \qquad v_i=\frac{w_i}{c_i}, \qquad
  u_i=\frac{w_i}{c_i\,\tau_i}.
\end{equation}
The next section builds a scheduler on these three numbers.

\section{Congestion-Priced Scheduling}\label{sec:sched}
% ===========================================================================

The formulation leaves the scheduler two numbers. Each suspended program
has a price per byte-second $u_i$ from~\eqref{eq:utility}, and each
replica has a shadow price $\theta$ for a byte-second of its memory.
Every KV decision compares the two. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cps} states the
scheduler; this section derives its steps and says what each one
guarantees.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Congestion-priced scheduling at one replica}
\label{alg:cps}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Observe} the prefill arrival rate $\lambda$, load $\rho$
  and mean wait $W$, and the memory $\Delta C$ that must be freed.
\STATE \textbf{Price} each suspended program: $\Phi_i$
  from~\eqref{eq:price} with $\Delta S_i$ from~\eqref{eq:prefill},
  $w_i=p_i\Phi_i$, $u_i=w_i/(c_i\tau_i)$.
\STATE \textbf{Set} $\theta$ to the smallest value at which the blocks
  with $u_i\le\theta$ free $\Delta C$; $\theta=0$ if nothing must be
  freed.
\STATE \textbf{Evict} the blocks with $u_i\le\theta$
  (Prop.~\ref{prop:memory}), or whole programs by the guarded rule
  (Prop.~\ref{prop:guarded}).
\STATE \textbf{Keep, offload or drop} each remaining state by the least
  of $\theta c_i\tau_i$, the priced fetch and $w_i$
  (\S\ref{sec:admission}).
\STATE \textbf{Place} a resuming turn at
  $\arg\min_j\,W_j+S_j+M_j+F_j$ with $M_j=\Phi_i$ at node~$j$'s load,
  or the priced fetch if the state is in a shared store
  (\S\ref{sec:routing}).
\STATE \textbf{Admit} a waiting session while the memory it will hold,
  at $\theta$ per byte-second, costs less than its wait.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The inputs are observable. The miss penalty $\Delta S_i$ comes from the
cost model~\eqref{eq:prefill} at the program's context length,
calibrated once per replica (\S\ref{sec:exp-calib}). The resume
probability $p_i$ and the expected remaining suspension $\tau_i$ come
from the program's phase and the tool it called (\S\ref{sec:sessions}).
The arrival rate, load and wait of the prefill queue are running
averages at the replica. The shadow price is the price of the cheapest
byte-second the replica would have to give up: zero while the pool has
room and the expected resident KV of \S\ref{sec:sessions} is below $M$,
and otherwise the $u_i$ of the marginal resident block. Prices change
with load, so a program's rank can change while it waits. A decision
made at one load should not be frozen into a key, which is the point
of pricing at decision time.

\subsection{Eviction}\label{sec:evict}

When memory runs out the controller must free $\Delta C$ bytes at the
least price,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:evict-priced}
  \min_{S}\ \sum_{i\in S}w_i \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{i\in S}c_i\ge\Delta C ,
\end{equation}
a covering knapsack, NP-hard in general and approximable to any factor
$1+\varepsilon$ \citep{csirik1991,carnes2008}. Two facts make it easy
in practice. Engines evict blocks rather than programs, so the problem
is nearly fractional, and a fractional knapsack is solved by price per
byte \citep{dantzig1957}. And the shadow price $\theta$ turns the
constraint into a threshold.

\begin{proposition}[Memory price and block-level eviction]\label{prop:memory}
Let $w_i\ge0$ be prices and $c_i>0$ sizes.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Threshold rule.} For any $\theta\ge0$, the set of states
  with $w_i/c_i\le\theta$ costs no more than any other set that frees at
  least as much memory.
\item \emph{Blocks.} Taking blocks in increasing order of $w_i/c_i$ and
  stopping at the first block $x$ that reaches the target costs at most
  the cost of any feasible set plus $w_x$.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{threshold\_rule\_optimal}, \leanref{density\_prefix\_plus\_one}}
\end{proposition}

At the $\theta$ that frees exactly $\Delta C$ the threshold rule is
optimal, and the same $\theta$ prices offloading and admission below.
Held for $\tau_i$ seconds, a byte of state costs $\theta\tau_i$, so
among states of equal price and size the one with the longer expected
tool call goes first. Where programs must be evicted whole,
price-per-byte order can fail by any factor, and a guard repairs it.

\begin{proposition}[Guarded program-level eviction]\label{prop:guarded}
Let $w_i\ge0$ and $c_i>0$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item Evicting whole programs in increasing order of $w_i/c_i$ until
  $\Delta C$ is freed has no constant approximation ratio
  for~\eqref{eq:evict-priced}.
\item For each program $e$, discard $e$ and every program with
  $w_j>w_e$, and evict $e$ together with the remaining programs in
  increasing order of $w_j/c_j$ until $\Delta C$ is freed. The cheapest
  such candidate costs at most twice any feasible set.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{densityFirst\_unbounded}, \leanref{threshold\_prefix\_le}, \leanref{guardedGreedy\_two\_approx}}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Fixed keys.} Deployed systems evict by a key fixed in
advance: the shortest context first \citep{thunderagent}, the least
recently used, the longest idle, or the expired time-to-live
\citep{li2025continuum,xia2026mori}. Each is Algorithm~\ref{alg:cps}
with $u_i$ replaced by a quantity that does not depend on whether the
program will resume, and that omission is what they pay for.

\begin{proposition}[Price-blind keys]\label{prop:blind}
Consider~\eqref{eq:evict-priced} with $w_i=p_i\Phi_i$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item Let a rule choose which of two programs to evict from any
  information except their resume probabilities. For every $R$ there
  are resume probabilities in $(0,1]$ under which the program it evicts
  costs more than $R$ times the other, while either program alone frees
  the target. No such rule has a constant approximation ratio
  for~\eqref{eq:evict-priced}.
\item If every program resumes and its price is its recompute work
  alone, so that $w_i=c_i^2$ by~\eqref{eq:prefill}, then shortest-first
  is the price-per-byte order: it frees $\Delta C$ whenever the pool
  holds it, costs at most twice any feasible set, and the factor~2 is
  tight.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{price\_blind\_rule\_unbounded}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_feasible}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_two\_approx}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_two\_approx\_tight}}
\end{proposition}

Part~(ii) is the regime in which shortest-first is defensible: light
prefill load, where by Proposition~\ref{prop:price}(ii) the price is
close to $\Delta S_i$, and programs that all resume. It needs no guard
there, because the last program it takes is never dearer than the
dearest program of any feasible set. Even there it is not optimal: with
contexts $4,5,6$ and a target of $6$ it evicts $\{4,5\}$ at cost~41
while $\{6\}$ is feasible at cost~36,\provedby{\leanref{shortestFirst\_not\_optimal}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_optimality\_claim\_false}}
contrary to the optimality theorem stated for this problem by
\citet[Def.~4.1, App.~F.3]{thunderagent}. Outside that regime
part~(i) applies, to shortest-first and to every other key that ignores
$p_i$. How far real traces are from uniform resume probabilities is
measured in \S\ref{sec:exp-evict}.

\subsection{Placement}\label{sec:routing}

A resuming turn can be served where its state is or elsewhere. The
router charges each node~$j$ the wait and service there, the migration
cost $M_j$ of moving or recomputing the state, and the expected future
cost $F_j$ of the program's remaining turns. With node~1 holding the
state ($M_1=0$), the rule $\min_j W_j+S_j+M_j+F_j$ keeps the turn at
node~1 iff
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lookahead}
  (W_1+S_1)-(W_2+S_2) < M_2+(F_2-F_1).
\end{equation}
\provedby{\leanref{lookahead\_prefers\_iff}}
The price of a miss supplies $M_j$: a move turns the next turn into a
miss served at node~$j$, so $M_j$ is $\Phi_i$ evaluated at node~$j$'s
prefill load, small at an idle node even for a long context and large
at a loaded one. With a shared KV store \citep{mooncake-vllm} the
state is fetched rather than recomputed, and $M_j$ is the priced
fetch of \S\ref{sec:admission}, smaller still. Session affinity,
the rule that never moves a program,
sets $M_j=\infty$ for every other node, and no finite migration cost
justifies that at every load.

\begin{proposition}[The limit of affinity]\label{prop:routing}
Let the affinity node be an M/M/1 queue with service rate~$\mu$ and let
moving to an idle node of the same speed cost $1/\mu+M+F$ with
$M,F\ge0$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item The response time of the affinity node exceeds $1/\mu+M+F$ iff
  $\lambda>\lambda^*=\mu-1/(1/\mu+M+F)$, a stable load in $[0,\mu)$
  whose utilisation is $\rho^*=\mu(M+F)/(1+\mu(M+F))$.
\item $\lambda^*$ is nondecreasing in $M$ and in~$F$. Hence for
  \emph{any} finite $M,F$ strict session affinity loses at some stable
  load, and a cheaper move lowers that load.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{affinity\_loses\_iff}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_stable}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_utilization}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_mono}, \leanref{affinity\_not\_always\_optimal}, \leanref{mm1Wait\_unbounded}}
\end{proposition}

The inversion load is set by the cost of a move in units of a service
time: a move costing four service times keeps affinity optimal up to
$80\,\%$ utilisation, one costing a quarter of a service time only up to
$20\,\%$.\provedby{\leanref{inversionLoad\_examples}} A shared KV store
moves a deployment from the first case towards the second, which is
why affinity and a shared store are alternatives rather than
complements. E5 measures the move cost and the inversion load
(\S\ref{sec:exp-routing}).

\subsection{Admission and Offloading}\label{sec:admission}

The memory pool couples the two roles of KV, and the coupling is
unstable. A miss holds its turn in the prefill queue and then in the
batch for longer, which holds more bytes, which evicts more suspended
state, which causes more misses. The simulation of \S\ref{sec:sim}
shows a replica switching between a hit rate near one and one near zero
under the same load. The shadow price gives the admission rule its
unit: a new session is admitted when the memory it will hold, at
$\theta$ per byte-second, costs less than the delay it avoids by not
waiting, and $\theta$ rises steeply as the pool fills. In practice this
is a cap on live sessions set where the expected resident KV of
\S\ref{sec:sessions} stays below the pool, and it is the decision that
keeps the eviction rules in the regime where they matter.

Between keeping and dropping lies offloading to a slower tier. A state
is kept, offloaded or dropped by the least of $\theta c_i\tau_i$, the
priced fetch and $w_i$, where the priced fetch is $\Phi_i$ with the
transfer time in place of $\Delta S_i$ when the fetch holds the prefill
stage, plus the tier's wait once it saturates. Since offloading only
enlarges the choice set, enabling it never raises the optimal cost,
while always offloading can be worse than never
offloading.\provedby{\leanref{optimal\_cost\_antitone\_in\_actions}, \leanref{enabling\_offload\_never\_hurts}, \leanref{always\_offload\_can\_be\_worse}}
The collapse of an always-offloading stack at high concurrency reported
by \citet[App.~A.2]{thunderagent} is therefore a property of that
policy, not of the mechanism, and the per-program choice above is what
avoids it.

\paragraph{Fixed rules as special cases.} Every rule in use replaces
one price by a constant. Shortest-first sets $w_i=c_i^2$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}(ii)); recency, idleness and time-to-live
keys set $p_i$ to a constant (Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}(i)); always
offloading sets the priced fetch to zero and never offloading to
infinity; strict affinity sets $M_j=\infty$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:routing}); admitting every session sets
$\theta=0$. Each is right in the load regime where its constant happens
to match the price, and \S\ref{sec:exp} asks how often that regime
occurs.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Decision accuracy is the primary metric: whether
the model picks the same policy as the real optimum and ranks policies
in the same order (Kendall~$\tau$ over policy sets, fraction of states
with the same $\argmin$), reported alongside MAPE of TTFT, TPOT and
throughput on held-out workloads. Error is decomposed by substituting
measured for modelled quantities one link of the chain at a time, and
out-of-distribution checks vary tool time, context skew, reuse rate and
program mix beyond the calibration range.

\paragraph{E1: Calibration.}\label{sec:exp-calib} We profile prefill
time over new tokens, cached prefix and batch, decode step time over
batch size and total batch KV, and transfer time over bytes per link,
and fit~\eqref{eq:prefill}, \eqref{eq:decode} and $\varphi(n)$. We also
measure the largest batch that fits in memory under the trace's
footprint distribution, which \S\ref{sec:batch} shows cannot be
inferred from the mean footprint.

\paragraph{E2: Variance and the price.}\label{sec:exp-variance}
\emph{Hypothesis:} the hit/miss mixture's share of per-turn prefill
$\mathrm{Var}[S]$ grows from the trace baseline of
\S\ref{sec:exp-traces} to a majority once eviction is active, the
prefill wait tracks $(1+\mathrm{CV}^2)/2$ with and without chunking,
and decode occupancy depends on decode load only. We decompose
$\mathrm{Var}[S]$ by the law of total variance into the append and the
mixture, vary the hit
rate by controlling eviction under both prefill schedulers, and force
misses on a controlled fraction of turns to compare the rise in summed
TTFT with Proposition~\ref{prop:price}(i).

\paragraph{E3: Offloading.}\label{sec:exp-offload} \emph{Hypothesis:}
the priced keep/offload/drop choice is never below never-offload, and
always-offload falls below it once the tier saturates, at the
concurrencies ThunderAgent reports; whether the stack fetches on the
critical path decides the ranking.

\paragraph{E4: Eviction and admission.}\label{sec:exp-evict}
\emph{Hypothesis:} on replayed eviction events, SF is within a factor~2
of the optimum when resume probabilities are uniform, price-per-byte
order beats it when they vary, the byte-second key $u_i$ beats both when
tool times differ, and the full price of Proposition~\ref{prop:price}
matters at prefill loads where the mean wait is comparable to a miss.
End to end, we sweep the admission cap, since the simulation shows it
decides whether eviction matters at all.

\paragraph{E5: Routing.}\label{sec:exp-routing} \emph{Hypothesis:}
strict affinity wins at low load and loses above a load that
\eqref{eq:lookahead} predicts from measured waits and priced $M_j$.

\paragraph{E6: Scorecard.}\label{sec:exp-scorecard} For each decision we
score the analytical model and the calibrated simulator against the
testbed with the protocol above.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Queueing foundations.} \citet{kleinrock1975}; the PK formula
and M/G/1 \citep{pollaczek1930,khinchine1932}; Little's law
\citep{little1961}; GI/G/1 bounds \citep{kingman1962}; SRPT optimality
\citep{schrage1968}; closed and interactive networks
\citep{lazowska1984}; product-form networks and insensitivity
\citep{bcmp1975,kelly1979}. Charging a job for the delay it imposes on
others is classical in the economics of queues
\citep{naor1969,mendelson1990}; there the price steers customers who
decide whether to join, whereas here it ranks the states a server
keeps. Knapsack relaxation and covering knapsack:
\citet{dantzig1957,csirik1991,carnes2008}.

\paragraph{Queueing models of LLM serving.} \citet{mitzenmacher2025queueing}
set out why LLM jobs, with variable inference times, growing memory
footprints and several preemption options, need new queueing models.
Existing analyses take the variability from output length:
\citet{yang2024mg1} use an M/G/1 model to show that capping the output
of a few requests cuts queueing delay, \citet{ozbas2026reasoning} let a
class mixture of reasoning budgets set the second moment, and
\citet{shahout2024trail} give a closed form for a memory-aware SRPT
variant. Fluid and stability analyses cover throughput:
work-conserving schedulers are throughput-optimal, also for agent
workloads with fork-join structure \citep{dai2025workconserving};
memory growth sets the equilibrium batch and stability region, from
which admission rules follow \citep{ao2025fluid,nie2026stability};
flow control, routing and prefill/decode contention have provable
guarantees \citep{dong2026flowcontrol,bari2025optimal,lin2026pdcontention}.
Closest to our memory results, \citet{ao2026congestion} show that the
eviction-free equilibrium of a memory-constrained replica is unstable
and converges to a limit cycle with large throughput loss; the
thrashing of \S\ref{sec:sim} is the same phenomenon with KV reuse across
turns. \citet{chen2026fleetsim} pair M/G/$c$ formulas with simulation
for capacity planning, as we do. None of these models KV reuse across
the turns of a session, so none prices a miss.

\paragraph{Scheduling theory with KV constraints.}
\citet{jaillet2025online} prove that no deterministic online algorithm
has a constant competitive ratio under arbitrary arrivals with KV
constraints and give one under conditions; \citet{feng2026nonclairvoyant}
give the first constant-competitive non-clairvoyant policy;
\citet{wang2025variable} show that first-come-first-served,
shortest-output-first and size-based priorities have unbounded
approximation ratios for batch scheduling under a KV budget, a result
in the spirit of our Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}(i) for eviction.
\citet{dexter2025prefix} prove NP-hardness of scheduling with prefix
reuse under TTFT constraints and study the trade-off between reuse and
fairness.

\paragraph{Agentic and multi-turn KV serving.} ThunderAgent
\citep{thunderagent} and InferCept \citep{infercept} manage the KV of
programs paused on external calls; InferCept, to our understanding,
scores preserve, discard and swap by the memory each wastes, where the
price of a miss scores by queueing delay. Continuum \citep{li2025continuum}
keeps a program's KV through its tool call for a time-to-live set from
the reload cost and the queueing delay an eviction would induce, a
heuristic form of \S\ref{sec:price}; MORI \citep{xia2026mori} offloads by
the idleness of tool calls and reports durations spanning orders of
magnitude, which gives $\tau_i$ its role. TokenCake
\citep{bian2025tokencake}, KVFlow \citep{pan2025kvflow} and CacheScout
\citep{zhang2026cachescout} predict reuse from the agent workflow;
TOPAS \citep{ni2026topas} states the trade-off between a retained prefix
and the batch memory it displaces, which \S\ref{sec:batch} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:memory} formalise.
KVLearn \citep{kvlearn2026} learns a retention score that weighs
recompute, transfer and storage costs and adapts its threshold from
observed hit rates and memory pressure; it is a learned counterpart of
the price~\eqref{eq:utility} and the shadow price~$\theta$, which we
derive instead and use to say when fixed rules fail.
Chunked prefill \citep{sarathi2024} protects decoding from long
prefills; that head-of-line blocking among prefills remains motivates
\citet{hsieh2026flowprefill}, \citet{liu2026chunkedfair} and
\citet{lyu2025fairbatching}, and it is the term our price keeps.
Production traces \citep{vllm-agentx}, distributed KV pools
\citep{mooncake-vllm} and PPD's append-prefill routing \citep{ppd}
supply the setting.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
% ===========================================================================

The propositions are about the model, not the world, and the
experiments of \S\ref{sec:exp-real} have not been run. The replica
model treats the prefill stage as a FIFO server with a fixed available
fraction, although the budget left by the decode batch fluctuates and
session feedback makes arrivals non-Poisson, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:pk} is an
approximation there; Kingman's bound says bursty arrivals add delay on
top of it. Decode capacity depends on the number of turns in the batch,
with the KV dependence of a step carried by~\eqref{eq:decode} alone. A
real batch limit admits turns in FIFO order and is not insensitive;
\S\ref{sec:sim} finds the error of the saturating $\varphi$ follows the
work $\mathrm{CV}^2$ near saturation. The propositions concern means,
while service objectives are tails, and they price an eviction as a
stationary fraction of misses, whereas a real eviction is one event
with a transient effect that the simulation shows can be a thrash. The
scheduler uses estimates of load, wait, $p_i$ and $\tau_i$; how much of
the guarantees survives these substitutions is what E4 tests. The
trace statistics of \S\ref{sec:exp-traces} describe two Claude Code
workloads, one interactive and one benchmark-driven; other agents and
harnesses may append and reuse differently, the prefill-work
$\mathrm{CV}^2$ there rests on an assumed cost model, and the proxy's
TTFT comes from a serving stack and load we could not observe. The
ThunderAgent statements we discuss were checked against
arXiv:2602.13692v3; we have not reproduced its experiments.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work provides analytical tools for capacity planning and scheduling
in LLM serving systems. Better utilisation of serving hardware lowers the
energy and cost of deploying language models; we see no direct negative
societal consequences beyond those generally associated with more
efficient LLM deployment.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:decode}]
(i) Write $a_n=1/\prod_{k\le n}\varphi(k)$, so
$L(\rho)=\sum_nna_n\rho^n/\sum_na_n\rho^n$. For $\rho_1\le\rho_2$,
\[
  L(\rho_2)-L(\rho_1)=\frac{\sum_{n,m}na_na_m(\rho_2^n\rho_1^m-\rho_1^n\rho_2^m)}{P(\rho_1)P(\rho_2)},
\]
with $P(\rho)=\sum_na_n\rho^n$. Exchanging $n$ and $m$ and averaging, the
numerator is half of
$\sum_{n,m}a_na_m(n-m)(\rho_2^n\rho_1^m-\rho_1^n\rho_2^m)$. For $n\ge m$
the last factor is $\rho_1^m\rho_2^m(\rho_2^{n-m}-\rho_1^{n-m})\ge0$, and
symmetrically for $n<m$ both factors are non-positive, so every term is
non-negative. This holds for every truncation of the sums at a finite
$N$, and $L$ is the limit of the truncated means, so it holds for $L$.
By insensitivity, $L$ depends on the policy only through $\rho$, and a
policy that adds expected work $A$ per arrival moves the load to
$\rho+\lambda A$.

(ii) With constant capacity $\pi$ is geometric and $L=\rho/(C-\rho)$.
With $\rho'=\rho+\lambda A$ and $A=\sum_iq_i\Delta S_i$,
\[
  L(\rho')-L(\rho)=\frac{C\,\lambda A}{(C-\rho)(C-\rho')}
  =\frac{C-\rho}{C-\rho'}\cdot\frac{C\,\lambda A}{(C-\rho)^2},
\]
and the last factor is $\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi^D_i$ (with $\Delta D_i$ in
place of $\Delta S_i$). Since $\rho'\ge\rho$ the prefactor is at least
one.

(iii) At $\rho=C-\varepsilon$, $\Phi^D_i=C\Delta D_i/\varepsilon^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:memory}]
(i) Let $T=\{i: w_i/c_i\le\theta\}$ and let $S$ free at least as much,
$c(T)\le c(S)$. Removing the common part gives
$c(T\setminus S)\le c(S\setminus T)$. Members of $T\setminus S$ have
$w_i\le\theta c_i$ and members of $S\setminus T$ have $w_i>\theta c_i$, so
$w(T)-w(S)=w(T\setminus S)-w(S\setminus T)\le\theta\bigl(c(T\setminus S)
-c(S\setminus T)\bigr)\le0$.

(ii) Let $P$ be the blocks taken before $x$ and $\theta=w_x/c_x$. Every
block in $P$ has price per byte at most $\theta$, every block outside
$P$ at least $\theta$, and $c(P)<\Delta C\le c(O)$ for any feasible $O$.
The exchange step of (i) gives $w(P)\le w(O)$, hence
$w(P)+w_x\le w(O)+w_x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:price}]
Write $u=1-\rho$, $m_2=\E[S^2]$,
$A=\sum_iq_i\Delta S_i$ and
$B=\sum_iq_i\bigl((S_i^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})^2\bigr)$.
The change raises $\E[S]$ by $A$ and $\E[S^2]$ by $B$, so the load
becomes $\rho'=\rho+\lambda A$ and $1-\rho'=u-\lambda A>0$. By
\eqref{eq:pk}, $L=\lambda^2m_2/(2u)+\rho$, hence
\[
  \Delta L=\lambda A+\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\Bigl(\frac{m_2+B}{u-\lambda A}
    -\frac{m_2}{u}\Bigr)
  =\lambda A+\frac{Y}{u(u-\lambda A)},
\]
with $Y=\lambda^2(uB+\lambda Am_2)/2\ge0$. Summing \eqref{eq:price} with
$W=\lambda m_2/(2u)$ gives $\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i=\lambda A+Y/u^2$.

(i) Since $0<u-\lambda A\le u$, $Y/(u(u-\lambda A))\ge Y/u^2$, which is
the lower bound. For the upper bound,
\[
  \frac{u}{u-\lambda A}\Bigl(\lambda A+\frac{Y}{u^2}\Bigr)-\Delta L
  =\frac{(\lambda A)^2}{u-\lambda A}\ \ge\ 0 .
\]

(ii) $(S^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S^{\mathrm{hit}})^2
=(S^{\mathrm{miss}}+S^{\mathrm{hit}})\Delta S$, so the second term of
$\Phi_i$ is $\lambda(S^{\mathrm{miss}}+S^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2u)$ times
$\Delta S_i$, and the third is $\lambda W\Delta S_i/u$. Dividing gives
both ratios.

(iii) Let $m_1=\E[S]$ and $\lambda=(1-\varepsilon)/m_1$ with
$0<\varepsilon\le1/2$, so $\rho=1-\varepsilon$ and $\lambda\ge1/(2m_1)$.
The first two terms of $\Phi_i$ are non-negative, and the third is
$\lambda^2m_2\Delta S_i/(2\varepsilon^2)\ge m_2\Delta S_i/(8m_1^2\varepsilon^2)$,
which exceeds any bound for small~$\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:guarded}]
(i) Take $\Delta C=2$ and three programs with $(w,c)=(0,1)$, $(K,K)$ and
$(2,1)$, $K\ge1$. Their prices per byte are $0\le1\le2$. The order takes
the first program, which frees~1, then the second, which completes the
target, at cost~$K$. The first and third programs also free~2, at
cost~2. Choose $K>2R$.

(ii) Let $O$ be any feasible set and $e$ its most expensive member. It
suffices to show that the candidate built for $e$ costs at most
$2w(O)$, since the rule returns the cheapest candidate. If
$c_e\ge\Delta C$ the candidate is $\{e\}$ and costs $w_e\le w(O)$.
Otherwise let the pool be the programs other than~$e$ with
$w_j\le w_e$. It contains $O'=O\setminus\{e\}$, which frees at least
$\Delta C-c_e$, so the greedy pass over the pool completes. Let it take
$P$ and then $x$, the program that completes the target, and let
$\theta=w_x/c_x$. Every member of $P$ has price per byte at most
$\theta$, every pool member outside $P$ at least~$\theta$, and
$c(P)<\Delta C-c_e\le c(O')$.

\emph{Exchange step.} Removing the common part $P\cap O'$ from both
sides of $c(P)\le c(O')$ gives $c(P\setminus O')\le c(O'\setminus P)$.
Then
\begin{align*}
  w(P)-w(O')&=w(P\setminus O')-w(O'\setminus P)\\
  &\le\theta\,c(P\setminus O')-\theta\,c(O'\setminus P)\ \le\ 0 .
\end{align*}
The candidate therefore costs at most
$w_e+w(P)+w_x\le w_e+w(O')+w_e=w(O)+w_e\le2w(O)$, using $w_x\le w_e$ and
$w_e\le w(O)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}]
(i) Take two programs of the same size $c>0$ that agree in everything
the rule observes, and a target $\Delta C=c$, so that either program
alone is feasible. The rule sees the same input whatever the resume
probabilities are, so it evicts a fixed one of the two, say $e$, and let
$o$ be the other. Set $p_e=1$ and $p_o=1/(|R|+2)$, both in $(0,1]$. The
evicted program costs $p_ec^2=c^2$, while $o$ costs
$c^2/(|R|+2)$, and $R\cdot c^2/(|R|+2)<c^2$ since $R<|R|+2$. The
optimum is at most the cost of $o$, so the rule's ratio exceeds~$R$.

(ii) By~\eqref{eq:prefill} the recompute work of a context of $c$
tokens is $ac+bc^2/2$; the proposition takes the quadratic term as the
price, $w_i=c_i^2$, so $w_i/c_i=c_i$ and price-per-byte order is
shortest-first (SF). SF frees $\Delta C$ whenever the pool holds it: it
stops only at a program at least as long as the remaining target or
when the pool is exhausted. For the ratio, let SF take
$t_1\le\dots\le t_m$, the first $m$ programs in sorted order. Write $L=t_m$ and $P=t_1+\dots+t_{m-1}$, so $P<\Delta C$ (SF did
not stop earlier). Let $S$ be any feasible set.

\emph{Step 1: $L^2\le\mathrm{cost}(S)$.} Every program with $c_i<L$ is
among the first $m-1$ in sorted order, and these free only $P<\Delta C$.
So $S$ contains a program with $c_i\ge L$, whose cost alone is at
least~$L^2$.

\emph{Step 2: a lower bound on $\mathrm{cost}(S)$.} Let
$f(x)=x(L-x)$ for $x<L$ and $f(x)=0$ otherwise, so $f\ge0$ on $[0,\infty)$
and $x^2\ge Lx-f(x)$ for all $x\ge0$ (with equality for $x<L$, and
$x^2\ge Lx$ for $x\ge L$). Summing over $S$ and using
$\sum_{i\in S}c_i\ge\Delta C$,
\[
  \mathrm{cost}(S)\ \ge\ L\,\Delta C-\sum_{i\in S}f(c_i)
  \ \ge\ L\,\Delta C-\sum_{j<m}t_j(L-t_j),
\]
because only programs with $c_i<L$ contribute to the sum, all of them
are among $t_1,\dots,t_{m-1}$, and $t_j(L-t_j)\ge0$ for $j<m$. The right
side equals $L(\Delta C-P)+\sum_{j<m}t_j^2$.

\emph{Step 3.} Since $\Delta C-P>0$,
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{cost}(\mathrm{SF})&=\textstyle\sum_{j<m}t_j^2+L^2\\
  &\le \mathrm{cost}(S)-L(\Delta C-P)+L^2\\
  &\le \mathrm{cost}(S)+L^2,
\end{align*}
and Step~1 bounds the last term by $\mathrm{cost}(S)$.

Tightness: on $K,K+1$ with $\Delta C=K+1$, SF takes $K$ (leaving $1$), then
$K+1$, at cost $K^2+(K+1)^2$, while $\{K+1\}$ costs $(K+1)^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:routing}]
An idle node of service rate $\mu$ has response time $1/\mu$, so moving
there costs $B=1/\mu+M+F>0$. (i) For $\lambda<\mu$ the M/M/1 response
time is $1/(\mu-\lambda)$, and $1/(\mu-\lambda)>B$ iff
$\mu-\lambda<1/B$ iff $\lambda>\mu-1/B=\lambda^*$. Since $B\ge1/\mu$,
$1/B\le\mu$ and $\lambda^*\ge0$; since $1/B>0$, $\lambda^*<\mu$.
Dividing by $\mu$ and writing $B=(1+\mu(M+F))/\mu$ gives
$\rho^*=1-1/(\mu B)=\mu(M+F)/(1+\mu(M+F))$. (ii) Increasing $M$ or $F$
increases $B$, decreases $1/B$ and so increases $\lambda^*$. Any
$\lambda\in(\lambda^*,\mu)$ is a stable load at which a policy that
always stays is beaten by one that moves.
\end{proof}
