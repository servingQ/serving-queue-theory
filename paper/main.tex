\begin{abstract}
Benchmarks of LLM serving systems report kernel latency and tokens per
second, but the latency users see is dominated by queueing once
utilisation approaches saturation. We model agentic serving as a chain of
dependent quantities. The KV eviction policy sets the prefix-cache hit
rate. The hit rate sets the service-time distribution. The distribution
sets utilisation and queueing delay. Delay enters the cost that routing
and eviction decisions minimise. On this chain we prove results for three
decisions an agentic serving system makes. Enabling KV offloading never
increases the optimal cost, although always offloading can.
Shortest-context-first eviction is not optimal; it is a 2-approximation
when every suspended program resumes, and arbitrarily bad when resume
probabilities differ. Strict session affinity is not optimal at high
load. These results explain, and delimit, two recently published claims
about agentic serving. Results on prefill/decode disaggregation are
collected in an appendix and will be developed in follow-up work. We then design experiments on a rack-scale NPU
testbed and a calibrated simulator whose success criterion is
\emph{decision faithfulness}: agreement with the real system on which
policy is better, rather than cycle-accurate latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Response time of a serving system decomposes as
\[
  T = W_q + S ,
\]
queueing delay plus service time. Hardware benchmarks measure~$S$; users
experience~$T$. Near saturation $W_q$ dominates, and it does so
\emph{non-linearly}: in the simplest Markovian model a 10\,\% increase in
arrival rate (from $\rho = 0.90$ to $\rho = 0.99$) multiplies response time
by ten (with $W=1/(\mu-\lambda)$ and $\mu=10$\,req/s, $W$ goes
from $1$\,s to $10$\,s).\provedby{\leanref{mm1Wait\_example\_ratio}} This single fact reframes most serving
questions. ``How many tokens/s does this NPU deliver?'' becomes ``at what
utilisation does p99~TTFT cross the SLA, and which control knob moves that
point?''

Agentic workloads sharpen the problem. In production coding-agent traces
the median session has 43 LLM turns, a median input of 142K tokens, a
median output of 444 tokens and a prefix-cache hit rate above 96\,\%
\citep{vllm-agentx}. Each turn appends a few hundred tokens to a context of
$10^5$ tokens, interleaved with tool calls of unpredictable duration. The
unit of work that matters is the \emph{program} (a multi-turn session with
persistent KV state), not the request \citep{thunderagent}. Whether a
program's KV cache is kept, offloaded or recomputed, and on which replica
the next turn runs, determines the service-time distribution that feeds
the queue.

This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A model} (\S\ref{sec:model}) that connects the eviction
policy, the hit rate, the service-time distribution, queueing delay and
the control cost. It is assembled from standard results (Little's law,
the Pollaczek--Khinchine formula, Kingman's bound, the interactive
response-time law), which we state and cite.
\item \textbf{Results for three decisions} (\S\S\ref{sec:congestion}--\ref{sec:routing}):
how the hit/miss mixture drives delay, when offloading helps, how to
choose what to evict, and when session affinity fails. Each result is
placed next to the published claim it bears on. Two claims from
ThunderAgent \citep{thunderagent} are re-examined: that KV offloading is
ineffective, and that shortest-context-first eviction is optimal. The
first holds only in an identifiable regime. The second is false, and we
bound how far from optimal the heuristic can be. Appendix~\ref{app:pd}
states our results on prefill/decode (PD) disaggregation, a third
ThunderAgent claim, which a follow-up paper will treat in detail.
\item \textbf{An experimental design} (\S\ref{sec:exp}) whose primary
metric is decision faithfulness. Result tables are laid out with
placeholders; no measurement is reported in this draft.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Conventions.} Propositions are our results; their proofs are
in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Theorems are standard results, cited and not
re-proved. When we discuss another paper we report what it
states, with section and figure, and we have not re-run its experiments.

\section{A Model of Agentic Serving}\label{sec:model}
% ===========================================================================

The model is a chain in which each quantity is set by the one before it:
\begin{multline*}
  \text{eviction policy} \to p \to \bigl(\E[S],\E[S^2]\bigr)
  \to \bigl(\rho,\E[W_q]\bigr) \\ \to \text{control cost}\ \eqref{eq:mdp}
  \to \text{policy}.
\end{multline*}
Here $p$ is the prefix-cache hit rate and $S$ the service time of a turn.
The rest of this section defines each link; \S\S\ref{sec:congestion}--\ref{sec:routing}
derive consequences.

\subsection{Congestion at a service centre}

Requests arrive at rate~$\lambda$ to a service centre (one replica, or one
device pool) with service time~$S$. Utilisation is $\rho=\lambda\E[S]$.
Three standard results relate these quantities to delay.

\begin{theorem}[Little's law; \citealp{little1961}]\label{thm:little}
In a stable system in which the long-run arrival rate $\lambda$ and the
mean time in system $W$ exist and are finite, the long-run mean number in
the system is $L=\lambda W$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Pollaczek--Khinchine; \citealp{pollaczek1930,khinchine1932}]\label{thm:pk}
In an M/G/1 FIFO queue with arrival rate $\lambda$, service time $S$ with
$\E[S^2]<\infty$, and $\rho=\lambda\E[S]<1$, the mean waiting time is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pk}
  \E[W_q] = \frac{\lambda \E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)} .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

For exponential service ($\E[S^2]=2\E[S]^2$) this gives the M/M/1 response
time $W=\E[W_q]+\E[S]=1/(\mu-\lambda)$ with $\mu=1/\E[S]$
\citep{kleinrock1975}. Agentic arrivals are not Poisson: turns of one
program are separated by tool calls and are correlated. Kingman's bound
covers general renewal arrivals and shows that arrival variability adds to
service variability rather than replacing it.

\begin{theorem}[Kingman's bound; \citealp{kingman1962}]\label{thm:kingman}
In a GI/G/1 FIFO queue with arrival rate $\lambda$, interarrival variance
$\sigma_a^2$, service variance $\sigma_s^2$ and $\rho<1$,
\[
  \E[W_q] \le \frac{\lambda(\sigma_a^2+\sigma_s^2)}{2(1-\rho)} .
\]
\end{theorem}

In both \eqref{eq:pk} and Theorem~\ref{thm:kingman}, service variance
enters linearly and the load enters through $1/(1-\rho)$. The model
therefore needs two things from the cache: the mean service time, which
sets~$\rho$, and its variance.

\subsection{KV reuse shapes the service time}

A turn whose prefix is resident in the KV cache (a hit) needs only an
append-prefill of the new tokens and a decode. A miss re-prefills the
whole context. With hit probability~$p$,
\begin{gather*}
  S = \begin{cases} S^{\mathrm{hit}} & \text{w.p. } p \\
                    S^{\mathrm{miss}} & \text{w.p. } 1-p, \end{cases}\\
  \E[S] = p\,S^{\mathrm{hit}} + (1-p)\,S^{\mathrm{miss}} .
\end{gather*}
The eviction and admission policy determines~$p$, hence $\E[S]$,
$\E[S^2]$ and~$\rho$.

What drives evictions is memory pressure, and Little's law gives it
directly. If programs start at rate $\lambda_{\mathrm{prog}}$ and each
stays resident for a time $T_{\mathrm{prog}}$ that includes its tool
calls, the mean number of resident programs is
$\lambda_{\mathrm{prog}}\E[T_{\mathrm{prog}}]$. If the KV footprint $K$ is
independent of residence time, the mean resident KV is
$\lambda_{\mathrm{prog}}\E[T_{\mathrm{prog}}]\E[K]$. Longer tool calls
therefore raise memory demand without raising compute demand. Once
resident KV exceeds device memory, the eviction policy has to choose what
to drop, and that choice sets~$p$.

\subsection{Programs as jobs in a closed network}

An agent program cycles through
$\text{prefill}\to\text{decode}\to\text{tool}\to\text{prefill}\to\cdots$.
Tool execution plays the role of \emph{think time} in an interactive closed
network \citep{lazowska1984}. A program in the tool phase is not
finished. It is suspended future work with resident state. Its phase
$z_p(t)\in\{\text{reasoning},\text{acting},\text{waiting}\}$ and the
transition probabilities $\Prob(\text{next phase}\mid z)$ are observable,
and a request-level scheduler discards them. Benchmarks that fix the
number of concurrent programs $N$ \citep{thunderagent} are closed systems,
to which the following law applies.

\begin{theorem}[Interactive response-time law; \citealp{lazowska1984}]\label{thm:irtl}
In a closed interactive system with $N$ jobs, mean think time $Z$ and
throughput $X$, the mean response time is $R = N/X - Z$.
\end{theorem}

With fixed per-visit service demands, the throughput of a closed
product-form network does not decrease as $N$ grows \citep{lazowska1984}.
A throughput that falls with $N$ therefore signals that the demand per
step grows with $N$. In our chain the mechanism is a falling hit rate:
more programs, more memory pressure, more misses, and each miss turns a
cheap append into a full re-prefill.

\subsection{Serving as a control problem}

Let $X_t = (Q_t, \mathrm{KV}_t, P_t, R_t)$ collect queue states, KV
placement, program states and resources. Actions are
$A_t \in \{\text{route},\text{evict},\text{offload},\text{migrate},
\text{admit},\text{prefetch}\}$. The serving problem is
\begin{multline}\label{eq:mdp}
  \min_\pi \E_\pi\Bigl[\sum_t
     \alpha\,\text{latency}_t + \beta\,\text{recompute}_t \\
   + \gamma\,\text{migration}_t + \delta\,\text{SLA-violation}_t \Bigr].
\end{multline}
We do not solve \eqref{eq:mdp}. We use it as a common objective against
which heuristics can be compared: each policy in
\S\S\ref{sec:kv}--\ref{sec:routing} is a one-step approximation of it,
and the propositions say when such an approximation is exact and when it
fails.

\section{Congestion and the Hit/Miss Mixture}\label{sec:congestion}
% ===========================================================================

Theorem~\ref{thm:pk} makes the second moment of service time, not only
its mean, a first-class quantity.

\begin{proposition}[Variance drives queueing delay]\label{prop:pk}
Let $S_A$ and $S_B$ have finite discrete distributions with equal mean
and $\mathrm{Var}[S_A]<\mathrm{Var}[S_B]$, and let $\E[W_q]$ be given
by~\eqref{eq:pk}. Then $\E[W_q^A]<\E[W_q^B]$ at every stable load.
\provedby{\leanref{pkWait\_lt\_of\_variance\_lt}}
\end{proposition}


Raising the hit rate lowers both moments at once.

\begin{proposition}[Cache reuse empties the queue]\label{prop:cache}
Let $0\le S^{\mathrm{hit}}\le S^{\mathrm{miss}}$, let a request hit with
probability~$p$, and let $\rho=\lambda\E[S]$ with $\lambda>0$. For
$p\le q$ in $[0,1]$, if the system is stable at hit rate~$p$, the PK
delay at~$q$ is at most the PK delay at~$p$.
\provedby{\leanref{meanService\_antitone}, \leanref{utilization\_antitone}, \leanref{secondMomentService\_antitone}, \leanref{pkWait\_mixture\_antitone}}
\end{proposition}


Because $\E[W_q]$ is convex in~$\rho$ and unbounded as $\rho\to1$, cache
reuse is more than a compute saving. It removes load at the point where
load is most expensive.

\paragraph{Where the variance comes from.} We do not assume that agentic
workloads have intrinsically high service-time variance. With a prefix hit
rate above 96\,\% \citep{vllm-agentx}, a turn is an append-prefill of a
few hundred tokens plus a decode of a few hundred tokens. If every turn
hit, $\E[S^2]$ would be close to $\E[S]^2$. Variance enters through the
hit/miss mixture. A miss re-prefills a $10^5$-token context and costs one
to two orders of magnitude more than a hit.

Relative to exponential service with the same mean and load,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cv2}
  \frac{\E[W_q]_{M/G/1}}{\E[W_q]_{M/M/1}}=\frac{1+\mathrm{CV}^2}{2},
  \qquad \mathrm{CV}^2=\frac{\E[S^2]}{\E[S]^2}-1 .
\end{equation}
With a 96\,\% hit rate, $S^{\mathrm{hit}}=50$\,ms and
$S^{\mathrm{miss}}=5$\,s (full re-prefill of a long context),
$\mathrm{CV}^2>15$, which is more than $8\times$ the delay of exponential
service. With the same hit rate and a cheap miss
($S^{\mathrm{miss}}=100$\,ms), $\mathrm{CV}^2<0.05$.
\provedby{\leanref{pkWait\_ratio\_to\_exponential}, \leanref{mixtureCV2\_agentic\_example}, \leanref{mixtureCV2\_cheap\_miss\_example}}
The variance is thus a property of the miss penalty and the eviction
policy, not of the workload label. A second, fleet-level source is mixing
request classes (short chat, long document, agent) in one FIFO queue.
That is the classical motivation for class separation and for
shortest-remaining-processing-time scheduling, which minimises mean
response time in a single-server queue \citep{schrage1968}. Which source
dominates in a given deployment is an empirical question
(\S\ref{sec:exp-variance}).

\section{Managing KV State: Offloading and Eviction}\label{sec:kv}
% ===========================================================================

Section~\ref{sec:congestion} implies that the objective of a KV policy is
not the hit ratio but $\E[\mathrm{TTFT}]$ or
$\Prob(\mathrm{TTFT} > \mathrm{SLA})$. A natural per-byte utility for
keeping program~$i$ resident is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility}
  U_i = \frac{\Prob(\text{reuse}_i)\cdot \Delta S_i}{\mathrm{KVBytes}_i}\cdot
        \frac{1}{1-\rho},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta S_i=S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}-S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}$. The last factor
makes resident state more valuable as the system approaches saturation.
This section asks two questions about a suspended program: whether to
move its KV to a slower tier, and which programs to evict when memory
runs out.

\subsection{Offloading}\label{sec:offload}

\paragraph{The claim.} ThunderAgent \citep[App.~A.2, Fig.~7]{thunderagent}
reports that vLLM$+$LMCache offloading collapses at high concurrency: 111,
54, 62 and 40 step/min with 88\,\%, 2\,\%, 1\,\% and 1\,\% hit rate at
24, 48, 72 and 96 parallel programs. It attributes this to PCIe
bandwidth.

\paragraph{The model.} An offloading tier is a second service centre with
capacity $B_{\mathrm{tier}}/\E[K]$ transfers per second. Once
$\lambda\,\Prob(\text{offload})\,\E[K] > B_{\mathrm{tier}}$ it saturates
and every miss pays the transfer. The falling throughput is also what
\S\ref{sec:model} predicts for a closed system whose hit rate collapses.
The observation is consistent with the model. What it supports is a
statement about a policy, not about a mechanism.

Offloading enlarges the action set of a KV controller from
$A_0=\{\text{keep},\text{recompute}\}$ to $A_1=A_0\cup\{\text{offload}\}$.
A minimum over a larger set is never larger, so \emph{enabling}
offloading never increases the optimal cost. \emph{Always} offloading
can still be strictly worse than the $A_0$ optimum, for instance when
$J(\text{keep})=1$, $J(\text{recompute})=5$ and
$J(\text{offload})=10$.\provedby{\leanref{optimal\_cost\_antitone\_in\_actions}, \leanref{enabling\_offload\_never\_hurts}, \leanref{always\_offload\_can\_be\_worse}}

\paragraph{Reading the claim.} Hence the
reported numbers show that \emph{always-offload} is a bad policy at that
operating point, not that enabling offloading is harmful. A controller
that chooses $a_i^\ast = \argmin\{J_{\mathrm{keep}},
J_{\mathrm{offload}}, J_{\mathrm{recompute}}\}$ per program can never do
worse than never offloading. Version~3 of ThunderAgent reaches the same
conclusion. Its Appendix~A.4 (added in v3) states that ThunderAgent ``is
orthogonal to KV-cache offloading'', demonstrates it on SGLang HiCache,
and reframes the finding as ``offloading alone is insufficient at high
concurrency''. The open question is \emph{program-aware selective
offloading} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:utility} evaluated per program
(\S\ref{sec:exp-offload}).

\subsection{Eviction}\label{sec:evict}

\paragraph{The claim.} ThunderAgent's Lemma~4.1 models recompute cost as
quadratic in context length. Its Definition~4.1 poses the eviction problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:evict}
  \min_{S}\ \sum_{i\in S}c_i^2 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{i\in S}c_i\ge\Delta C
\end{equation}
over subsets $S$ of suspended programs with context lengths $c_i$ and a
memory target $\Delta C$. Its Appendix~F.3 states a theorem that selecting
the programs with the smallest~$c_i$ is optimal.

\begin{proposition}[Shortest-context-first eviction]\label{prop:evict}
Let shortest-first (SF) scan the programs in increasing order of $c_i$ and
evict until $\Delta C$ is freed.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item SF is not optimal for~\eqref{eq:evict}: for $c=\{4,5,6\}$ and
      $\Delta C=6$, SF evicts $\{4,5\}$ at cost~41 while $\{6\}$ is
      feasible at cost~36.
\item SF is a 2-approximation for~\eqref{eq:evict}: for every instance
      with $\Delta C>0$ and every feasible $S$, the SF cost is at most
      twice the cost of~$S$. The factor~2 is tight: for $c=\{K,K+1\}$ and
      $\Delta C=K+1$ the ratio is $(K^2+(K+1)^2)/(K+1)^2\to2$.
\item If program~$i$ resumes with probability $p_i$, the expected cost of
      evicting it is $p_ic_i^2$, and SF has no constant approximation
      ratio: for every $R$ there is a two-program instance in which SF
      costs more than $R$ times a feasible alternative.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{shortestFirst\_not\_optimal}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_optimality\_claim\_false}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_feasible}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_two\_approx}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_two\_approx\_tight}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_unbounded\_with\_resume\_prob}}
\end{proposition}


Problem~\eqref{eq:evict} is a covering (min-)knapsack, NP-hard in general
and approximable to any factor $1+\varepsilon$ in polynomial time
\citep{csirik1991,carnes2008}. Its continuous relaxation is solved by a
classical greedy rule.

\begin{theorem}[Greedy solves the knapsack relaxation; \citealp{dantzig1957}]\label{thm:dantzig}
The linear relaxation $\max\sum_iv_iy_i$ s.t.\ $\sum_ic_iy_i\le B$,
$y\in[0,1]^n$, with $v_i\ge0$, $c_i>0$, is solved by filling items in
decreasing order of $v_i/c_i$, with at most one fractional item.
\end{theorem}

Substituting $y_i=1-x_i$ turns the relaxation of \eqref{eq:evict} with
costs $w_i$ into this form with $B=\sum_ic_i-\Delta C$. The relaxed
eviction problem is therefore solved by evicting in \emph{increasing}
order of the cost per byte freed, $w_i/c_i$. With $w_i=p_ic_i^2$ this
density is $p_ic_i$, not $c_i$. When all $p_i$ are equal the two orders
coincide, which is why SF loses at most a factor~2 in
Proposition~\ref{prop:evict}(ii); when they differ, SF sorts by the wrong
key.

\paragraph{Reading the claim.} Proposition~\ref{prop:evict}(i) refutes the
Appendix~F.3 theorem as stated. Its proof is an exchange argument on
$(c_{\mathrm{short}}+r)^2 > c_{\mathrm{short}}^2 + r^2$ that replaces a
long program by a short one ``and theoretically the residue $r$''. The
decision variable is a \emph{subset of programs}, and the residue is not a
program that can be selected. The argument is valid for the relaxation,
where Theorem~\ref{thm:dantzig} applies, and not for Definition~4.1. The
paper's own ablation (App.~G.3, v3) shows SF beating longest-first and
random eviction by about $2\times$ in throughput. That is consistent with
Proposition~\ref{prop:evict}(ii): with uniform resume behaviour SF is a
good heuristic. The ablation does not compare against the optimum or
against the density rule. How much resume probabilities vary in real
agent traces decides which regime of Proposition~\ref{prop:evict}
applies, and is measured in \S\ref{sec:exp-evict}.

\section{Program-Aware Routing}\label{sec:routing}
% ===========================================================================

A myopic router sends a turn to the node minimising $W_j + S_{ij}$. A
KV-aware router adds a locality term. A program-aware router also charges
the migration cost $M_j$ and the expected future cost $F_j$ of the
program's remaining turns, a one-step approximation of
$\E[V(X_{t+1})\mid p,j]$ in~\eqref{eq:mdp}. Session affinity (always stay
where the state is) is the extreme case $M_j=\infty$ for every other node.
Let node~1 hold a program's state and node~2 be a candidate. With
$M_1=0$, the lookahead rule $\min_j W_j+S_j+M_j+F_j$ prefers node~1 iff
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lookahead}
  (W_1+S_1)-(W_2+S_2) < M_2+(F_2-F_1).
\end{equation}
\provedby{\leanref{lookahead\_prefers\_iff}}

\begin{proposition}[Program-aware routing and the limit of affinity]\label{prop:routing}
For \emph{any} finite $M,F\ge0$ there is a stable load $\lambda<\mu$ at
which the M/M/1 response time of the affinity node exceeds $1/\mu+M+F$,
the cost of moving to an idle node of the same speed. Hence strict
session affinity is not an optimal policy.
\provedby{\leanref{affinity\_not\_always\_optimal}, \leanref{mm1Wait\_unbounded}}
\end{proposition}

Session affinity and KV-aware routing are both one-step approximations
of~\eqref{eq:mdp}. Proposition~\ref{prop:routing} says that the
approximation which ignores queueing entirely fails at high load.
Appendix~\ref{app:pd} applies the comparison~\eqref{eq:lookahead} to prefill and decode pools.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:exp-protocol}

An analytical model will not match the system cycle for cycle. The
question is which discrepancies matter for the decisions the model is
used to make. We assess three levels of validity.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textbf{Structural validity.} The mechanisms that drive decisions
are represented: queue state, KV locality and transfer cost, memory
pressure, program phase transitions, and the dependence of service time
on cached tokens.
\item \textbf{Calibration.} Service and transfer models are fitted from
profiling, not assumed (E1).
\item \textbf{Held-out validation.} On workloads not used for calibration
we report \emph{metric accuracy} (MAPE of TTFT, TPOT, p99 and throughput),
\emph{trend accuracy} (sign of the response to load, hit rate and tool
time) and \emph{decision accuracy} (whether the model picks the same
policy as the real optimum and ranks policies in the same order).
\end{enumerate}
Decision accuracy is primary. A model that predicts a TTFT reduction of
$430\to370$\,ms when the system shows $500\to420$\,ms is 14\,\% off in
level but right about the decision, and the decision is what a scheduler
consumes. We report policy-ranking agreement (Kendall~$\tau$ over policy
sets) and optimal-decision agreement (fraction of states where the
$\argmin$ coincides) alongside MAPE. Error is decomposed as
$E_{\text{service}} + E_{\text{queue}} + E_{\text{network}} +
E_{\text{program}}$ by substituting measured for modelled quantities one
link of the chain at a time, and each cost term of~\eqref{eq:mdp} is
ablated to show the decision accuracy it contributes. Out-of-distribution
checks vary tool time ($2\to10$\,s), context skew, reuse rate and program
mix beyond the calibration range.

The model does not represent collective-communication contention,
kernel-launch jitter, allocator fragmentation or network head-of-line
blocking. Where those dominate we expect error to grow and will report
it.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
% ===========================================================================

The propositions are about the model, not the world. Their empirical
force depends on the experiments of \S\ref{sec:exp}, which have not been
carried out. Propositions~\ref{prop:pk} and~\ref{prop:cache} assume Poisson
arrivals and a single server. Agentic arrivals are bursty and correlated,
and Theorem~\ref{thm:kingman} shows that arrival variability adds to the
delay rather than cancelling service variability, so the PK formula can
underestimate it. We have not measured the service-time variance of a
real agentic deployment; \S\ref{sec:congestion} argues where it should
come from, and E2 measures it. The
ThunderAgent statements we discuss were checked against
arXiv:2602.13692v3; we have not reproduced its experiments.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work provides analytical tools for capacity planning and scheduling
in LLM serving systems. Better utilisation of serving hardware lowers the
energy and cost of deploying language models; we see no direct negative
societal consequences beyond those generally associated with more
efficient LLM deployment.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
% ===========================================================================

\sloppy
Every proposition and numerical claim in the paper has also been
formalised and machine-checked in Lean~4 with Mathlib; the development is
in the supplementary material. The proofs below are written for human
readers and do not follow the formal proofs step by step.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:pk}]
Let $S$ take value $s_i$ with probability $q_i$, and let
$m=\E[S]=\sum_iq_is_i$. Expanding the square,
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Var}[S]&=\sum_iq_i(s_i-m)^2\\
&=\textstyle\sum_iq_is_i^2-2m\sum_iq_is_i+m^2\sum_iq_i\\
&=\E[S^2]-m^2 .
\end{align*}

Hence $\E[S_A^2]=\mathrm{Var}[S_A]+m^2<\mathrm{Var}[S_B]+m^2
=\E[S_B^2]$ when the means are both $m$. The loads are equal because
$\rho=\lambda m$ in both cases, and for fixed $\lambda>0$ and $\rho<1$
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pk} is linear in $\E[S^2]$ with slope
$\lambda/(2(1-\rho))>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:cache}]
Write $h=S^{\mathrm{hit}}$ and $m=S^{\mathrm{miss}}$.

For $p\le q$,
$\E[S](q)-\E[S](p)=(q-p)(h-m)\le0$ because $h\le m$. The same computation
with $h^2$ and $m^2$ gives the claim for $\E[S^2]$, since $0\le h\le m$
implies $h^2\le m^2$, and $\rho=\lambda\E[S]$ with $\lambda>0$ is
non-increasing too.

The PK delay at hit rate $x$ is $\lambda\E[S^2](x)/(2(1-\rho(x)))$.
For $q\in[0,1]$ both terms of $\E[S^2](q)=qh^2+(1-q)m^2$ are
non-negative, so the numerator at $q$ is non-negative, and it is
at most the numerator at $p$. Also $\rho(q)\le\rho(p)<1$, so the
denominator at $q$ is positive and at least the denominator at $p$. A
non-negative numerator that shrinks over a positive denominator that
grows gives a smaller ratio.

The numbers around Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv2}: for exponential service $\E[S^2]=2\E[S]^2$, so the
ratio of PK expressions at equal $\lambda$ and $\rho$ is
$\E[S^2]/(2\E[S]^2)=(1+\mathrm{CV}^2)/2$. With $p=0.96$, $h=0.05$ and
$m=5$: $\E[S]=0.248$, $\E[S^2]=1.0024$ and $\mathrm{CV}^2\approx15.3$. With
$m=0.1$: $\E[S]=0.052$, $\E[S^2]=0.0028$ and $\mathrm{CV}^2\approx0.036$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:evict}]
(i) On $4,5,6$ with $\Delta C=6$, SF takes $4$ (so $2$ remains), then $5$,
and stops. Its cost is $16+25=41$, while $\{6\}$ frees $6$ at cost $36$.

(ii) Let SF take $t_1\le\dots\le t_m$, the first $m$ programs in sorted
order. Write $L=t_m$ and $P=t_1+\dots+t_{m-1}$, so $P<\Delta C$ (SF did
not stop earlier). Let $S$ be any feasible set.

\emph{Step 1: $L^2\le\mathrm{cost}(S)$.} Every program with $c_i<L$ is
among the first $m-1$ in sorted order, and these free only $P<\Delta C$.
So $S$ contains a program with $c_i\ge L$, whose cost alone is at
least~$L^2$.

\emph{Step 2: a lower bound on $\mathrm{cost}(S)$.} Let
$f(x)=x(L-x)$ for $x<L$ and $f(x)=0$ otherwise, so $f\ge0$ on $[0,\infty)$
and $x^2\ge Lx-f(x)$ for all $x\ge0$ (with equality for $x<L$, and
$x^2\ge Lx$ for $x\ge L$). Summing over $S$ and using
$\sum_{i\in S}c_i\ge\Delta C$,
\[
  \mathrm{cost}(S)\ \ge\ L\,\Delta C-\sum_{i\in S}f(c_i)
  \ \ge\ L\,\Delta C-\sum_{j<m}t_j(L-t_j),
\]
because only programs with $c_i<L$ contribute to the sum, all of them
are among $t_1,\dots,t_{m-1}$, and $t_j(L-t_j)\ge0$ for $j<m$. The right
side equals $L(\Delta C-P)+\sum_{j<m}t_j^2$.

\emph{Step 3.} Since $\Delta C-P>0$,
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{cost}(\mathrm{SF})&=\textstyle\sum_{j<m}t_j^2+L^2\\
  &\le \mathrm{cost}(S)-L(\Delta C-P)+L^2\\
  &\le \mathrm{cost}(S)+L^2,
\end{align*}
and Step~1 bounds the last term by $\mathrm{cost}(S)$.

Tightness: on $K,K+1$ with $\Delta C=K+1$, SF takes $K$ (leaving $1$), then
$K+1$, at cost $K^2+(K+1)^2$, while $\{K+1\}$ costs $(K+1)^2$.

(iii) Take $\Delta C=2$, program~A with $c=2$, $p=1$, and program~B with
$c=M\ge2$, $p=1/M^3$. SF evicts~A at expected cost $4$; evicting~B frees
enough memory at expected cost $M^2/M^3=1/M$. Choosing $M>R/4$ and
$M\ge2$ makes $4>R/M$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:routing}]
An idle node of service rate $\mu$ has response time $1/\mu$, so moving
there costs $B=1/\mu+M+F$. The M/M/1 response time $W(\lambda)=1/(\mu-\lambda)$
is unbounded on $[0,\mu)$: the load $\lambda=\mu-\varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon=\min(\mu,1/(|B|+2))$ gives $W(\lambda)=1/\varepsilon>B$. At that load a policy that
always stays is beaten by one that moves.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:pd}]
(i) Let $m=\min(N_P/s_P,N_D/s_D)$. Then $ms_P\le N_P$ and $ms_D\le N_D$.
Adding, $m(s_P+s_D)\le N_P+N_D=N$, i.e.\ $m\le N/(s_P+s_D)$.

(ii) At $N_P=Ns_P/(s_P+s_D)$ and $N_D=Ns_D/(s_P+s_D)$ both arguments of
the minimum equal $N/(s_P+s_D)$, so the bound in (i) is attained and no
split does better.

(iii) A number is below a minimum of four terms exactly when it is below
each of them. With $g_P=g_D=1$ and $I=0$ the compute term is
$N/(s_P+s_D)=\mu_A$, and the strict inequality cannot hold, which is (i)
again.
\end{proof}

\section{Prefill/Decode Disaggregation}\label{app:pd}
% ===========================================================================

This appendix collects our results on prefill/decode (PD)
disaggregation. They use the model of \S\ref{sec:model} and are proved
in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. A fuller treatment, including batching
inside each pool, is left to follow-up work.

\subsection{Static PD splits}\label{sec:pd}

\paragraph{The claim.} ThunderAgent \citep[App.~A.2, Fig.~7]{thunderagent}
reports that static PD splits of 1:3, 2:2 and 3:1 reach 87.1, 160.1 and
183.2 step/min, against 214.9 for aggregated serving. It attributes the
gap to a smaller effective HBM pool for prefill (``PD disaggregation
exacerbates thrashing'').

\paragraph{The model.} We treat each pool as a capacity and ask when a
split beats aggregation.

\begin{proposition}[PD disaggregation: no-gain theorem and exact win condition]\label{prop:pd}
Let $N$ identical devices serve requests with prefill work $s_P>0$ and
decode work $s_D>0$ (device-seconds). Ideal aggregation has capacity
$\mu_A=N/(s_P+s_D)$; a static split $N_P+N_D=N$ has capacity
$\mu_{PD}=\min(N_P/s_P,\,N_D/s_D)$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item $\mu_{PD}\le\mu_A$ for every split.
\item Equality holds at the rate-matched split $N_P=Ns_P/(s_P+s_D)$,
      which is therefore optimal.
\item Let aggregation pay an interference overhead~$I$, let dedicated pools
      realise specialisation gains $g_P,g_D$, and let PD be further capped
      by KV-transfer capacity $B_{\mathrm{net}}/\E[K]$ and memory caps
      $\mu_{P,\mathrm{mem}},\mu_{D,\mathrm{mem}}$. Then
      \begin{multline*}
        \mu_{PD} > \mu_A \iff
        \frac{N}{s_P+s_D+I} < \\
        \min\Bigl[\tfrac{N}{s_P/g_P+s_D/g_D},\;
                  \tfrac{B_{\mathrm{net}}}{\E[K]},\;
                  \mu_{P,\mathrm{mem}},\;\mu_{D,\mathrm{mem}}\Bigr],
      \end{multline*}
      i.e.\ PD wins iff \emph{each} of its four bottlenecks exceeds the
      aggregated capacity. With $g_P=g_D=1$ and $I=0$ the compute term
      equals $\mu_A$, recovering~(i).
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{pd\_le\_agg}, \leanref{pd\_eq\_agg\_at\_rate\_match}, \leanref{rate\_match\_optimal}, \leanref{pd\_beats\_agg\_iff}, \leanref{pdCompute\_eq\_agg\_of\_no\_gain}}
\end{proposition}


\paragraph{Reading the claim.} Proposition~\ref{prop:pd}(i)--(ii) says the
reported result is what the model predicts under the paper's conditions:
same hardware, no phase specialisation ($g_P=g_D=1$), and a memory-bound
prefill pool (a smaller HBM pool thrashes sooner, i.e.\
$\mu_{P,\mathrm{mem}}$ binds). Nothing specific to agentic workloads is
needed; the bound is a rate-matching identity. Proposition~\ref{prop:pd}(iii)
gives the condition under which the conclusion flips. PD is deployed in
practice \citep{vllm-agentx,mooncake-vllm} with long-context prefill
parallelism ($g_P>1$), wide expert-parallel decode ($g_D>1$), measurable
prefill/decode interference ($I>0$), a fast interconnect
($B_{\mathrm{net}}/\E[K]$ not binding), and a shared KV tier that removes
$\mu_{P,\mathrm{mem}}$ as a constraint. That is the regime the condition
identifies. The supported statement is regime-dependent: static PD
without specialisation and with a memory-bound prefill pool cannot beat
aggregation. The generalisation that PD does not help agentic serving is
not supported.

A second issue is the request shape. For turn $t\ge2$ a request is
``$10^5$ cached tokens $+\,500$ new tokens''. Routing it through the
prefill pool and shipping the whole KV to a decode node is often the
wrong path. Static PD is therefore the wrong abstraction for multi-turn
programs. The routing comparison of \S\ref{sec:routing} makes the
per-turn alternative precise.

\subsection{Per-turn routing between pools}\label{sec:pd-append}

Applied to PD pools, the comparison~\eqref{eq:lookahead} decides where a follow-up turn
is prefilled. Let $W_P,S_P$ be the prefill pool's wait and append service,
$T_{\mathrm{KV}}$ the transfer of the program's KV to the decode node,
$W_D$ the decode wait, $S_D$ the append service on the decode node, and
$I$ the interference it inflicts on co-located decodes.

The decode-local path costs $W_D+S_D+I$ and the prefill-pool path costs
$W_P+S_P+T_{\mathrm{KV}}+W_D$. The decode wait cancels, so decode-local
processing of turn $t\ge2$ is cheaper iff
\begin{equation}\label{eq:append}
  S_D+I < W_P+S_P+T_{\mathrm{KV}} .
\end{equation}
\provedby{\leanref{append\_prefill\_rule}}


Rule~\eqref{eq:append} is the append-prefill rule of PPD
\citep{ppd} written as a cost comparison. Together with
Proposition~\ref{prop:pd} it says that the useful form of PD for agentic
serving is \emph{dynamic}: first-turn prefills go to the prefill pool, and
follow-up turns are routed per turn by comparing the two sides of
\eqref{eq:append}. PPD-style routing is, like session
affinity and KV-aware routing, a one-step approximation
of~\eqref{eq:mdp}.

we compare the simulated winner (aggregation, or the best integer split)
with the condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:pd}(iii). Cells within
3\,\% of the boundary are skipped. The two agree in \simPdAgree{} of
\simPdCells{} decisive cells. The scalar model says nothing about
latency. Table~\ref{tab:sim-pd} shows that at the rate-matched split,
where the capacities are equal, disaggregated serving has a higher mean
latency, and the gap grows with load. A single pool of $N$ servers
queues less than a tandem of two smaller pools, a classical pooling
effect. A comparison of PD and aggregation at equal capacity therefore
has to report latency as well as throughput.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The PD model treats capacity as a scalar
bottleneck and ignores batching dynamics inside each pool. The simulator
does not model memory caps on PD pools.
