\begin{abstract}
An agentic LLM server keeps the KV cache of suspended programs across
tool calls; every KV decision asks what losing one costs. We model
sessions looping through turns and tools on replicas with a prefill
queue, a decode batch and one memory pool, and derive the \emph{price
of a miss}: the TTFT one miss adds over all turns. Beyond its
recompute, a miss holds up every prefill queued behind it, a quadratic
term chunked prefill does not remove; the decode stage prices only
work. One shadow price of memory then decides eviction, offloading,
placement and admission; deployed keys are special cases. With few
sessions live the queue is finite-source; the open wait bounds its
Markovian form. On an NPU testbed replaying production
coding-agent sessions a follow-up miss costs \eTwoMissOverHitLo{} to
\eTwoMissOverHitHi{} times a hit; the wait is for memory, not compute,
and the cap on live sessions decides it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Response time is queueing delay plus service time, $T=W_q+S$.
Hardware benchmarks measure~$S$; users experience~$T$. Near saturation
$W_q$ dominates and grows without bound: in the simplest Markovian
model, raising the load from $\rho=0.90$ to $0.99$ multiplies response
time by ten.\provedby{\leanref{mm1Wait\_example\_ratio}} The serving
question is therefore not how many tokens per second a device delivers
but which control knob moves the point where TTFT crosses its SLA.

Agentic workloads sharpen the problem. In production coding-agent traces
the median session has \wkTurnsMedian{} LLM turns over a context of
\wkContextMedian k tokens, and almost every turn is an append onto a
resident prefix (\S\ref{sec:exp-traces}; \citealp{vllm-agentx}). The unit of work that
matters is the \emph{program}, a multi-turn session with persistent KV
state, not the request \citep{thunderagent}. While a program waits for
a tool, its KV occupies device memory without doing work.

Every KV decision in such a system asks one question: what does it cost
to lose a suspended program's state? The obvious answer is the time to
recompute it. In a queue that answer is incomplete. A miss is long work
added to a shared server, and every other request pays for it. How much
they pay depends on how loaded the server already is and on whether the
miss shares the batch or blocks it.

Queueing models of LLM serving have so far asked when a server is
stable and which schedulers are throughput-optimal, with output length
as the source of variability
\citep{yang2024mg1,dai2025workconserving,ao2025fluid,nie2026stability}.
Agentic serving systems, for their part, keep or evict the KV of
suspended programs by rules of thumb such as recency, time-to-live or
idleness \citep{li2025continuum,xia2026mori,thunderagent}. We connect
the two. The variability that matters here is in the prefill work, and
it has two sources: the size of the append, which the scheduler does
not control, and the hit/miss mixture that the KV policy creates, which
it does. The second can be priced per miss, and the price is the
decision variable the policy needs.

This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A formulation and the price of a miss} (\S\ref{sec:model}).
Sessions arrive as a Poisson process and loop through turns and tools.
A replica has a prefill queue that serves pending prefills in order, a
decode batch that shares bandwidth, and one memory pool. A miss changes
only the prefill work, and we derive its price at the prefill queue as
the marginal externality of an M/G/1 job whose size grows
(Proposition~\ref{prop:price}); the decode stage is insensitive and
prices only work (Proposition~\ref{prop:decode}). Within a replica the
price is monotone in the context, so the load changes the order of
programs only through their resume probabilities and suspension times,
and it can reverse that order. With $N$ sessions live the prefill queue
is a finite-source system, and for its Markovian form we prove that
the open-queue wait at the same utilisation is an upper bound
(Proposition~\ref{prop:finite}); no policy change raises the mean
number in the stage by more than $N-L_P$.
\item \textbf{Congestion-priced scheduling} (\S\ref{sec:sched}). One
price per suspended program, its expected price per byte-second of
memory, is the variable shared by eviction, offloading, placement and
admission, compared with one shadow price of memory; the head-of-line
term of that price is what chunked prefill does not remove, and the
insensitivity of the decode stage is what keeps the miss out of it.
The eviction step is the covering knapsack, whose threshold rule and
guarded greedy are known and cited. A key blind to resume probabilities
has no bounded approximation ratio, with shortest-first as the special
case in which the price is proportional to the square of the context
(Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}), although in the traces we measured it
is the suspension time, not the resume probability, that separates
programs.
\item \textbf{Evidence} (\S\ref{sec:exp}). Production traces give the
workload side: the append alone has a prefill-work $\mathrm{CV}^2$ of
\wkCvN, resume probabilities vary little and suspension times vary
widely. In simulation the admission cap, not the eviction order,
decides whether a replica thrashes (the priced order beats
shortest-first only within seed noise), and on replayed production
sessions the open price overstates the rise that forced misses cause
by \simPriceOverMin{} to \simPriceOverMax{} times while the Markovian
finite-source price understates it. On an NPU testbed
(\S\ref{sec:exp-testbed}) the fitted prefill cost is quadratic past
\eOneKc k tokens, and a replay of a coding-agent trace shows the
follow-up miss costing \eTwoMissOverHitLo{} to \eTwoMissOverHitHi{}
times the hit, with a wait for KV space rather than prefill compute
that the cap on live sessions controls; the remaining experiments
(Appendix~\ref{app:design}) are pending.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Conventions.} Propositions are our results; their proofs are
in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Standard results are cited and not
re-proved. When we discuss another paper we report what it states, with
section and figure, and we have not re-run its experiments.

\subsection{Sessions, Turns and Tools}\label{sec:sessions}\label{sec:closed}

An agent program, or session, cycles through
$\text{prefill}\to\text{decode}\to\text{tool}\to\text{prefill}\to\cdots$.
Sessions arrive in a Poisson process of rate~$\Lambda$; inside a
session the loop is closed: a turn is served, its tool call takes a
random time, and with probability~$p_i$ the session issues another
turn. Tool time is the \emph{think time} of an interactive network
\citep{lazowska1984}, and a session in a tool call is suspended future
work with resident state. Turns reach the replicas at rate
$\lambda=\Lambda\,\E[\text{turns per session}]$, correlated within a
session. For networks of processor-sharing and delay stations the
BCMP theorem gives a product-form stationary law \citep{bcmp1975}, so
the decode stage can be analysed as if fed by Poisson arrivals at its
own rate whatever the tool-time law; for the FIFO prefill stage we use
the same reading as an approximation and test it in \S\ref{sec:sim}.

\subsection{A Replica: Two Resources and a Memory Pool}\label{sec:batch}\label{sec:queue}

A turn with context $K_i$ appends $n_i$ tokens and decodes $o_i$. It
makes three demands on its replica.
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Prefill compute.} Prefilling $n$ tokens onto a resident
  prefix of $K$ costs
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:prefill}
    P(n,K)=a\,n+b\,n\,(K+n/2),
  \end{equation}
  a linear term for the dense layers and an attention term over the
  prefix. A hit costs $P(n_i,K_i)$; a miss re-prefills the context,
  $P(K_i+n_i,0)$, quadratic in $K_i$, as ThunderAgent's Lemma~4.1 also
  assumes \citep{thunderagent}.
\item \emph{Decode bandwidth.} Each decode step reads the weights once
  for the batch and the KV of every turn in it, so decoding $o_i$ tokens
  takes
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:decode}
    D_i = o_i\bigl(\beta K_i+\omega/n\bigr)
  \end{equation}
  seconds of bandwidth, with $n$ the batch size, $\beta$ the time to
  read one token's KV and $\omega$ the time to read the weights. At
  agentic context lengths the KV term dominates, and it is the same for
  a hit and a miss.
\item \emph{Memory.} $c_i=\kappa K_i$ bytes for as long as the state is
  resident, running or suspended, with $\kappa$ the KV bytes per token.
\end{itemize}
The hit/miss distinction lives entirely in the first demand.

\paragraph{Decode stage.} Continuous batching advances every turn in
the batch at each iteration, so decoding turns share the device. We
model the decode stage as a processor-sharing (PS) station with
capacity $\varphi(n)$ when $n$ turns share it, in units
of~\eqref{eq:decode}; batching makes $\varphi$ increase with $n$ and
memory makes it saturate. The stationary law of the batch size is
$\pi(n)\propto\rho_D^n/\prod_{k\le n}\varphi(k)$ with
$\rho_D=\lambda\E[D]$, and it is \emph{insensitive}: it depends on the
demand only through its mean \citep{bcmp1975,kelly1979}.

\paragraph{Prefill stage.} Chunked prefill adds prefills to the batch
in near-equal chunks ``without pausing ongoing decodes''
\citep{sarathi2024}: each step first serves the decode batch, then
spends the tokens left in its budget on pending prefills. Chunking
protects the decoding turns from a long prefill; it does not let one
prefill overtake another, and by default the schedulers we know of
serve pending prefills in arrival order. We therefore model the prefill
stage as a FIFO queue with service $P_i$ whose server is available a
fraction $1-\rho_D$ of the time. For Poisson arrivals to a FIFO server
with load $\rho<1$ and $\E[S^2]<\infty$ (M/G/1) the
Pollaczek--Khinchine (PK) formula gives the mean wait
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pk}
  \E[W_q] = \frac{\lambda \E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)}
\end{equation}
\citep{pollaczek1930,khinchine1932}. Session feedback makes arrivals
non-Poisson and the budget fluctuates, so~\eqref{eq:pk} is an
approximation here. With $N$ sessions live the queue is a finite-source
system, and a session that is waiting cannot generate its next turn:
Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} shows that the open formula then bounds
the Markovian wait from above; Table~\ref{tab:sim-finite} puts the
ratio at $\simFiniteRatioMax\times$ for $N=\simFiniteNMin$ and
$\simFiniteRatioMin\times$ for $N=\simFiniteNMax$ at utilisation
$\simFiniteRho$. The two stages thus differ in what they need
from the cache: the decode stage needs only mean demands, which the
cache does not change; the prefill stage needs the second moment of its
work, which the cache sets.

\paragraph{Memory pool.} The batch and the suspended states share one
pool of $M$ bytes, so a replica admits turns into the batch until the
next one does not fit. The batch that fits is a property of the
footprint law and the admission rule, not of the mean footprint (a
more variable footprint can shrink the batch or enlarge
it),\provedby{\leanref{footprint\_variance\_hurts}, \leanref{footprint\_variance\_helps}}
so $\varphi$ is calibrated (Appendix~\ref{app:design}), not derived. Every
byte a suspended session holds is a byte the batch cannot use, and once
resident KV exceeds the pool the policy has to choose what to drop,
which sets the hit rate~$p$. A shared KV store \citep{mooncake-vllm}
adds a slower tier that every replica can read, so a state that is not
resident on the serving replica is fetched instead of recomputed.

\subsection{Prefill Work under KV Reuse}\label{sec:congestion}

With hit probability~$p$ the prefill work of a turn is the mixture
$S=S^{\mathrm{hit}}=P(n_i,K_i)$ with probability $p$ and
$S=S^{\mathrm{miss}}=P(K_i+n_i,0)$ otherwise, so the KV policy sets
$\E[S]$, $\E[S^2]$ and~$\rho_P$. At the FIFO prefill stage,
\eqref{eq:pk} makes the second moment a first-class quantity: of two
work laws with the same mean, the one with the larger variance waits
longer at every stable load,\provedby{\leanref{pkWait\_lt\_of\_variance\_lt}}
and raising the hit rate lowers both moments at once, so the PK wait is
non-increasing in~$p$.\provedby{\leanref{meanService\_antitone}, \leanref{utilization\_antitone}, \leanref{secondMomentService\_antitone}, \leanref{pkWait\_mixture\_antitone}}

We do not assume that agentic workloads have intrinsically high
variance; we ask where it comes from. Table~\ref{tab:cv2} splits
$\mathrm{Var}[S]$ by the law of total variance into the spread of the
appends within hits and within misses and the hit/miss mixture between
them. With every turn a hit, as the prefix hashes of the production
traces say $\wkReuse\,\%$ of their tokens would be, the append alone
gives $\mathrm{CV}^2=\wkCvN$ under a linear cost model, because tool
outputs range from a line to a file and a context compaction rewrites
the prompt. Forcing $1$ to $10\,\%$ of turns to miss adds the mixture,
which then supplies $\wkMixLinMin$ to $\wkMixLinMax\,\%$ of the
variance under the linear model and $\wkMixAttMin$ to
$\wkMixAttMax\,\%$ once attention dominates, so the append is never
less than $\wkAppendShareMin\,\%$ of the variance at these hit rates.
The mixture rivals or exceeds the appends only once eviction is
active (\simTraceTightMixMin{} to \simTraceTightMixMax\,\% at hit rates
of \simTraceTightHitMin{} and above in the replay of \S\ref{sec:sim}).
What the price of a miss must capture is therefore not a ratio of
$\mathrm{CV}^2$ but the rise of $\lambda\E[S^2]/(2(1-\rho))$ that one
more miss causes.

\subsection{The Price of a Miss}\label{sec:problem}\label{sec:price}

At a decision epoch each replica holds a set of suspended programs.
Program~$i$ holds $c_i$ bytes, resumes with probability~$p_i$ after an
expected further $\tau_i$ seconds, and its next turn needs prefill
$S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}$ if the state is resident on the replica that
serves it and $S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}=S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}+\Delta S_i$ if it
must be recomputed; with a shared store a remote state costs a transfer
instead. The controller keeps, offloads or drops each state, places
each resuming turn, and admits sessions, subject to resident bytes at
most~$M$ per replica, to minimise $L=L_P+L_D$ of Table~\ref{tab:model},
which by Little's law is the delay accumulated per unit time.
Relaxing the memory constraint with a multiplier $\theta\ge0$, the
shadow price of a byte-second, gives an additive cost: keeping
program~$i$ costs $\theta c_i\tau_i$, and dropping it costs the price
of the miss it may cause. In the stationary model a policy fixes which
fraction of arrivals are misses, so we ask what happens to $L_P$ when a
fraction $q_i$ of arrivals, belonging to program~$i$, turn from hits
into misses.

\begin{proposition}[The price of a miss]\label{prop:price}
Model the prefill stage as an M/G/1 FIFO server with arrival rate
$\lambda\ge0$, load $\rho<1$ and mean wait $W=\E[W_q]$
from~\eqref{eq:pk}. Turn a fraction $q_i\ge0$ of arrivals from hits of
work $S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}\ge0$ into misses of work
$S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}\ge S_i^{\mathrm{hit}}$ such that the new load
$\rho'=\rho+\lambda\sum_iq_i\Delta S_i$ is below one, and let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:price}
  \Phi_i=\Delta S_i
   +\frac{\lambda\bigl((S_i^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})^2\bigr)}{2(1-\rho)}
   +\frac{\lambda W\,\Delta S_i}{1-\rho}.
\end{equation}
Then the change in $L_P$ satisfies
$\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i\le\Delta L_P\le\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho'}\,
\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i$.
\provedby{\leanref{missPrice\_lower}, \leanref{missPrice\_upper}, \leanref{missPrice\_aggregate\_exact}}
\end{proposition}

$\Phi_i$ is the stationary marginal price: the time to first token,
summed over all turns, that one more miss of program~$i$ adds. Its
three terms are three ways a miss hurts. The miss itself takes longer
to prefill. Turns that arrive while it is being prefilled wait for all
of it, and that wait grows with the square of the miss less the square
of the hit, as the second moment in~\eqref{eq:pk} does; this is the
delay a job imposes on the others at a FIFO queue, the externality of
the economics of queues \citep{naor1969,mendelson1990}, here applied to
a job whose size changes. Its extra work raises the prefill load, which
stretches every wait already in the queue by $1/(1-\rho)$. The sum of
these prices never overstates the damage and understates it only by the
factor $(1-\rho)/(1-\rho')$, close to one when the change in load is
small. Which term dominates depends on the load: the second term is
$\lambda(S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}+S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2(1-\rho))$ times the
first and $(S_i^{\mathrm{miss}}+S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2W)$ times the
third,\provedby{\leanref{missPrice\_own\_vs\_queue\_ratio}, \leanref{missPrice\_queue\_terms\_ratio}}
so when few turns arrive during a miss the price is close to
$\Delta S_i$, when many arrive the turns queued behind it dominate, and
once the mean wait exceeds half the miss the load term takes over and
diverges as the stage approaches capacity.\provedby{\leanref{missPrice\_unbounded}}
Applied to one eviction rather than to a stationary fraction of misses,
$\Phi_i$ is an approximation, and \S\ref{sec:sim} measures how loose.

Two facts about how $\Phi_i$ orders programs follow from
\eqref{eq:prefill}. Within a replica, at one load and at equal append,
$\Phi_i$ is nondecreasing in the context $K_i$, since
$\Delta S_i=aK_i+bK_i^2/2$ and every term of \eqref{eq:price} grows
with it,\provedby{\leanref{prefillWork\_miss\_delta}, \leanref{missPrice\_mono\_context}}
so the load alone never reorders programs by their price; it reorders
them by their price per byte-second once resume probabilities or
suspension times differ. Under the cost model of the replay in
\S\ref{sec:sim}, a $200$k-token program resuming with probability
$0.3$ and a $20$k-token program resuming with probability $0.9$, both
appending $1000$ tokens, have the opposite order at an idle queue and
at $\rho=0.5$ with a two-second
wait,\provedby{\leanref{price\_order\_flips\_with\_load}} which no
key fixed in advance reproduces.

The M/G/1 model has one more assumption that a replica with long
contexts violates: an unbounded population. A pool of a few million
tokens holds about ten sessions of the workload in
\S\ref{sec:exp-traces}, and a session that is waiting for prefill
cannot submit its next turn. The Markovian finite-source model makes
the correction exact.

\begin{proposition}[Finite population]\label{prop:finite}
Let $N\ge1$ sessions each think for a time of mean $Z>0$ and then
submit a turn of exponential work with mean $1/\mu$ to one FIFO server.
The mean number at the server with $n$ sessions is given exactly by
mean value analysis \citep[\S6]{lazowska1984} as $Q_0=0$,
$Q_{n}=n\,(1+Q_{n-1})/(\mu Z+1+Q_{n-1})$; with $N$ sessions the
utilisation is $\rho_N=N/(\mu Z+1+Q_{N-1})<1$ and the mean wait in
queue is $W_N=Q_{N-1}/\mu$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item $Q_n$ is nondecreasing in $n$, $Q_n\le n$, and $Q_n$ is
  nonincreasing in $\mu Z$.
\item $W_N\le\rho_N/(\mu(1-\rho_N))$, the open M/M/1 wait at the same
  utilisation.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{mvaQ\_mono}, \leanref{mvaQ\_le\_card}, \leanref{mvaQ\_anti\_c}, \leanref{finite\_source\_rho\_lt\_one}, \leanref{finite\_source\_wait\_le\_open}}
\end{proposition}

The think-time law enters only through $Z$, since the delay station
is insensitive. Part~(ii) says that the wait $W$ that enters the price
of Proposition~\ref{prop:price} does not understate the Markovian
finite-source wait; with two sessions and $Z=2/\mu$ the open wait is
$4.5$ times the finite-source
wait,\provedby{\leanref{finite\_source\_two\_sessions\_example}} and
Table~\ref{tab:sim-finite} shows the ratio falling to
$\simFiniteRatioMin$ at $N=\simFiniteNMax$. Since at most $N$ turns
exist, a policy change at a replica with $N$ live sessions raises $L_P$
by at most $N-L_P$, whatever the work
law,\provedby{\leanref{closed\_price\_cap}} and the last part of~(i)
gives the finite-source price of longer work: the rise of $Q_N$ when
the mean work grows. Whether the open price or that finite-source
price is closer to the rise a real workload shows, with its
non-exponential appends and misses, is what the replay of
\S\ref{sec:sim} measures.

\begin{proposition}[The decode stage prices only work]\label{prop:decode}
Consider the PS decode stage of \S\ref{sec:batch} with any nondecreasing
capacity~$\varphi$ and load $\rho_D<\sup_n\varphi(n)$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item $L_D$ is nondecreasing in $\rho_D$. Hence, at every load, a policy
  whose turns add no more expected demand than another's leaves no more
  turns in the batch.
\item For constant capacity $C$ and added demand $\lambda\sum_i q_i\Delta D_i$
  raising the load to $\rho_D'<C$, the change in $L_D$ is exactly
  $\frac{C-\rho_D}{C-\rho_D'}\,\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi^D_i$ with
  $\Phi^D_i=C\,\Delta D_i/(C-\rho_D)^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{stationaryMean\_mono}, \leanref{ps\_eviction\_rank\_by\_work}, \leanref{psNum\_diff\_exact}, \leanref{psPrice\_lower}, \leanref{psPrice\_upper}, \leanref{psPrice\_unbounded}}
\end{proposition}

A miss adds no decode demand, so to first order the decode stage is
untouched by it; the load factor of part~(ii) is what a decision that
adds decode demand, such as admitting a session, pays. The prefill
queue is where a miss is paid for, with a head-of-line term that
batching does not remove, and the decode batch is where the memory a
kept state occupies would otherwise buy capacity. We define the
expected price of dropping program~$i$'s state, its price per byte and
its price per byte-second held as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility}
  w_i = p_i\,\Phi_i, \qquad v_i=\frac{w_i}{c_i}, \qquad
  u_i=\frac{w_i}{c_i\,\tau_i}.
\end{equation}

\section{Congestion-Priced Scheduling}\label{sec:sched}
% ===========================================================================

The formulation leaves the scheduler two numbers. Each suspended program
has a price per byte-second $u_i$ from~\eqref{eq:utility}, and each
replica has a shadow price $\theta$ for a byte-second of its memory.
Every KV decision compares the two. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cps} states the
scheduler; this section derives its steps.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Congestion-priced scheduling at one replica}
\label{alg:cps}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Observe} the prefill arrival rate $\lambda$, load $\rho$
  and mean wait $W$, and the memory $\Delta C$ that must be freed.
\STATE \textbf{Price} each suspended program: $\Phi_i$
  from~\eqref{eq:price} with $\Delta S_i$ from~\eqref{eq:prefill},
  $w_i=p_i\Phi_i$, $u_i=w_i/(c_i\tau_i)$.
\STATE \textbf{Set} $\theta$ to the smallest value at which the blocks
  with $u_i\le\theta$ free $\Delta C$; $\theta=0$ if nothing must be
  freed.
\STATE \textbf{Evict} the blocks with $u_i\le\theta$, or whole
  programs by the guarded greedy (\S\ref{sec:evict}).
\STATE \textbf{Keep, offload or drop} each remaining state by the least
  of $\theta c_i\tau_i$, the priced fetch and $w_i$
  (\S\ref{sec:admission}).
\STATE \textbf{Place} a resuming turn at
  $\arg\min_j\,W_j+S_j+M_j+F_j$ with $M_j=\Phi_i$ at node~$j$'s load,
  or the priced fetch if the state is in a shared store
  (\S\ref{sec:routing}).
\STATE \textbf{Admit} sessions up to the count at which the expected
  resident KV of \S\ref{sec:admission} fills the pool; hold the rest in
  an entry queue.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The inputs are estimates. The miss penalty $\Delta S_i$ comes from the
cost model~\eqref{eq:prefill} at the program's context length,
calibrated once per replica (\S\ref{sec:exp-real}). The resume
probability $p_i$ and the expected remaining suspension $\tau_i$ are
estimated from the program's phase, its tool and the gap so far
(\S\ref{sec:evict}). The arrival rate, load and wait of the prefill
queue are running averages at the replica. The shadow price is the
price of the cheapest byte-second the replica would have to give up:
zero while the pool has room, and otherwise the $u_i$ of the marginal
resident block. Prices change with load, so a program's rank can change
while it waits,\provedby{\leanref{price\_order\_flips\_with\_load}}
which is the point of pricing at decision time.

\subsection{Eviction}\label{sec:evict}

When memory runs out the controller must free $\Delta C$ bytes at the
least price,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:evict-priced}
  \min_{S}\ \sum_{i\in S}w_i \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{i\in S}c_i\ge\Delta C ,
\end{equation}
a covering knapsack, NP-hard in general and approximable within a
constant factor \citep{csirik1991,carnes2008}. Two known facts make it
easy in practice. Engines evict blocks rather than programs, so the
problem is nearly fractional, and the fractional knapsack is solved by
price per byte \citep{dantzig1957}: for any $\theta\ge0$ the set of
states with $w_i/c_i\le\theta$ costs no more than any other set that
frees at least as much memory, and taking blocks in increasing
$w_i/c_i$ until the target is reached costs at most any feasible set
plus the last
block.\provedby{\leanref{threshold\_rule\_optimal}, \leanref{density\_prefix\_plus\_one}}
The same holds per byte-second, with $c_i\tau_i$ in place of $c_i$
and a target in byte-seconds,\provedby{\leanref{threshold\_rule\_optimal\_byte\_seconds}}
which is the order Algorithm~\ref{alg:cps} uses; its target is the
bytes to free now, so ordering by $u_i$ against a byte target is a
heuristic mapping of that rule, and the simulation tests it
(\S\ref{sec:sim}). Where programs must be evicted whole,
price-per-byte order can fail by any factor, and the standard repair is
to guess the dearest program $e$, exclude every program dearer than $e$
from the pool, and run the greedy from $e$; the cheapest such candidate
costs at most twice the optimum \citep{csirik1991}, for any prices
$w_i$.\provedby{\leanref{densityFirst\_unbounded}, \leanref{threshold\_prefix\_le}, \leanref{guardedGreedy\_two\_approx}}

\paragraph{Fixed keys.} Deployed systems evict by a key fixed in
advance: the shortest context first \citep{thunderagent}, the least
recently used, the longest idle, or the expired time-to-live
\citep{li2025continuum,xia2026mori}. Each is Algorithm~\ref{alg:cps}
with $u_i$ replaced by a quantity that does not depend on $p_i$.

\begin{proposition}[Price-blind keys]\label{prop:blind}
Consider~\eqref{eq:evict-priced} with $w_i=p_i\Phi_i$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
\item Let a rule choose which of two programs to evict from any
  information except their resume probabilities. For every $R$ there
  are resume probabilities in $(0,1]$ under which the program it evicts
  costs more than $R$ times the other, while either program alone frees
  the target. No such rule has a constant approximation ratio
  for~\eqref{eq:evict-priced}.
\item If every program resumes and its price is its recompute work
  alone, so that $w_i\propto c_i^2$ by~\eqref{eq:prefill}, then shortest-first
  is the price-per-byte order: it costs at most twice any feasible set,
  and the factor~2 is tight.
\end{enumerate}
\provedby{\leanref{price\_blind\_rule\_unbounded}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_feasible}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_two\_approx}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_two\_approx\_tight}}
\end{proposition}

Part~(ii) is the regime in which shortest-first is defensible: light
prefill load, where the price is close to $\Delta S_i$, and programs
that all resume. Even there it is not optimal: with contexts $4,5,6$
and a target of $6$ it evicts $\{4,5\}$ at cost~41 while $\{6\}$ is
feasible at cost~36,\provedby{\leanref{shortestFirst\_not\_optimal}, \leanref{shortestFirst\_optimality\_claim\_false}}
contrary to the optimality theorem stated for this problem by
\citet[Def.~4.1, App.~F.3]{thunderagent}. Outside that regime part~(i)
applies to every key that ignores $p_i$. How much $p_i$ and $\tau_i$
vary is a workload question, and the production traces of
\S\ref{sec:exp-traces} answer part of it (Table~\ref{tab:resume} in
Appendix~\ref{app:traces}): the probability of another turn within ten
minutes is between \wkPmin{} and \wkPmax{} whether one conditions on
the turn index or on the preceding gap, while the expected gap varies
from \wkTauMin{} to \wkTauMax{}\,s. In these traces the byte-second
denominator of $u_i$, not $p_i$, is what separates programs, so a
time-to-live key is closer to the price than a shortest-first key, and
the adversary of part~(i) is a statement about what a key cannot see,
not about how often it is fooled.

\subsection{Placement}\label{sec:routing}

A resuming turn can be served where its state is or elsewhere. The
router charges each node~$j$ the wait and service there, the migration
cost $M_j$ of moving or recomputing the state, and the expected future
cost $F_j$ of the program's remaining turns. With node~1 holding the
state ($M_1=0$), the rule $\min_j W_j+S_j+M_j+F_j$ keeps the turn at
node~1 iff, by definition,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lookahead}
  (W_1+S_1)-(W_2+S_2) < M_2+(F_2-F_1).
\end{equation}
\provedby{\leanref{lookahead\_prefers\_iff}}
The price of a miss supplies $M_j$: a move turns the next turn into a
miss served at node~$j$, so $M_j$ is $\Phi_i$ at node~$j$'s prefill
load, small at an idle node even for a long context and large at a
loaded one. Session affinity, the rule that
never moves a program, sets $M_j=\infty$ for every other node. Against
an idle node of the same speed, moving costs $1/\mu+M+F$, and the
M/M/1 response time of the affinity node exceeds it exactly when its
utilisation exceeds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhostar}
  \rho^*=\frac{\mu(M+F)}{1+\mu(M+F)},
\end{equation}
which is nondecreasing in $M$ and
$F$.\provedby{\leanref{affinity\_loses\_iff}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_stable}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_utilization}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_mono}, \leanref{affinity\_not\_always\_optimal}, \leanref{mm1Wait\_unbounded}, \leanref{inversionLoad\_examples}}
The idle alternative gives the earliest inversion, so $\rho^*$ is a
lower bound on the load at which affinity loses to any real
alternative. A shared KV store \citep{mooncake-vllm} fetches the state
instead of recomputing it, so $M_j$ becomes the priced fetch of
\S\ref{sec:admission} and that load falls; affinity and a store are
alternatives rather than complements in the simulated configurations
of \S\ref{sec:sim}.

\subsection{Admission and Offloading}\label{sec:admission}

The memory pool couples the two roles of KV, and the coupling is
unstable: a miss holds its turn in the prefill queue and then in the
batch for longer, which holds more bytes, which evicts more suspended
state, which causes more misses. \citet{ao2026congestion} show the same
instability for a memory-constrained replica without reuse, and
\S\ref{sec:sim} shows it with reuse. Admission is the decision that
keeps the pool out of that regime. Each live session holds KV of size
$K(t)$ that grows with its age~$t$; treating live sessions as an
M/G/$\infty$ system, Campbell's theorem \citep{kingman1993} gives the
resident KV mean $\Lambda\,\E[\int_0^DK(t)\,dt]$ over the session
lifetime~$D$, which exceeds the naive $\Lambda\,\E[D]\,\E[K]$ whenever
long contexts belong to long sessions (by $\wkKvRatio\times$ in the
production traces, where the two correlate at $\wkCorrDK$, and by
$\trKvRatio\times$ in our SWE-bench Pro runs, where they correlate at
$\trCorrDK$). The
admission step caps live sessions where this estimate fills the pool,
and \S\ref{sec:sim} shows that the cap, not the eviction key, decides
whether the replica thrashes. The shadow price of the cap is the entry
wait, and a session is worth admitting while the memory it will hold,
at $\theta$ per byte-second, costs less than that wait.

Between keeping and dropping lies offloading to a slower tier. A state
is kept, offloaded or dropped by the least of $\theta c_i\tau_i$, the
priced fetch and $w_i$, where the priced fetch is $\Phi_i$ with the
transfer time in place of $\Delta S_i$ when the fetch holds the prefill
stage, plus the tier's wait once it saturates. Since offloading only
enlarges the choice set, enabling it never raises the optimal cost,
while always offloading can be worse than never
offloading.\provedby{\leanref{optimal\_cost\_antitone\_in\_actions}, \leanref{enabling\_offload\_never\_hurts}, \leanref{always\_offload\_can\_be\_worse}}
The collapse of an always-offloading stack at high concurrency reported
by \citet[App.~A.2]{thunderagent} is therefore a property of that
policy, not of the mechanism. Every rule in use replaces one price by a
constant: shortest-first sets $w_i\propto c_i^2$, recency and time-to-live
keys set $p_i$ to a constant, always offloading sets the priced fetch
to zero, strict affinity sets $M_j=\infty$, and admitting every session
sets $\theta=0$.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

Charging a job for the delay it imposes on others is classical in the
economics of queues, for joining \citep{naor1969} and for priority
pricing at the M/M/1 queue \citep{mendelson1990}; the size-squared
term of~\eqref{eq:price} is the PK second moment of that externality.
We claim the application (a job whose size changes with a cache
decision), the two-stage separation, the finite-population bound and
the one price across four decisions, not the formula.
Existing LLM queueing analyses \citep{mitzenmacher2025queueing} take
the variability from output length: \citet{yang2024mg1} cap the output
of a few requests, \citet{ozbas2026reasoning} let a class mixture of
reasoning budgets set the second moment, and \citet{shahout2024trail}
give a memory-aware SRPT variant; reordering prefills would change the
head-of-line term, not remove the miss. Closest to our memory results,
\citet{ao2026congestion} show the eviction-free equilibrium of a
memory-constrained replica unstable, converging to a limit cycle: the
thrashing of \S\ref{sec:sim} without reuse. None of these models KV reuse across
the turns of a session, so none prices a miss. Under KV constraints, \citet{jaillet2025online} and
\citet{feng2026nonclairvoyant} give competitive online policies,
\citet{wang2025variable} show size-based priorities to have unbounded
approximation ratios (the spirit of Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}(i)),
and \citet{dexter2025prefix} prove scheduling with prefix reuse
NP-hard.

Among systems, ThunderAgent \citep{thunderagent} and InferCept
\citep{infercept} manage the KV of programs paused on external calls
(InferCept, to our understanding, by the memory each option wastes).
Continuum \citep{li2025continuum} keeps a program's KV for a
time-to-live set from the reload cost and the queueing delay an
eviction would induce, the price of a miss without its head-of-line
term and load factor. MORI
\citep{xia2026mori} offloads by the idleness of tool calls, which
gives $\tau_i$ its role; TokenCake, KVFlow, CacheScout and TOPAS
\citep{bian2025tokencake,pan2025kvflow,zhang2026cachescout,ni2026topas}
predict reuse from the workflow or trade a retained prefix against
batch memory. KVLearn \citep{kvlearn2026} learns a
retention score from recompute, transfer and storage costs with an
adaptive threshold: the inputs of $u_i$ and $\theta$, to which the
analysis adds the form of the price and its bracket. Chunked
prefill \citep{sarathi2024} protects decoding from long prefills; the
head-of-line blocking among prefills that remains
\citep{hsieh2026flowprefill,liu2026chunkedfair,lyu2025fairbatching} is
the term our price keeps.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
% ===========================================================================

The propositions are about the model, not the world, and of the
measurements of Appendix~\ref{app:design} only E1 and one E2 sweep
have been run (one stack and workload, single seeds). The prefill
stage is a FIFO server with a fixed available fraction, although the
budget the decode batch leaves fluctuates and session feedback makes
arrivals non-Poisson; \eqref{eq:pk} is an approximation there, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} and the replay of \S\ref{sec:sim} show
the open queue overstating the wait when few sessions are live, a
regime the memory model implies whenever contexts are long. The
finite-source bound is proved for exponential work; for a real
workload the open price and the Markovian finite-source price bracket
the observed rise from above and below, so a policy change raises
$L_P$ by at most $\min(\mathrm{hi},N-L_P)$; the price inside that
range is open. The propositions concern means, not tails, and price an eviction as a
stationary fraction of misses, not as the transient event it is. The trace statistics describe
two Claude Code workloads (other harnesses append with
$\mathrm{CV}^2$ of $2$ to $7$), and the simulator shares the model's
abstractions (no block-level allocation beyond tail blocks, no
cross-session hits, one capacity function for prefill and decode).
What would falsify the model: a prefill wait that does not follow
$\lambda\E[S^2]/(2(1-\rho))$ once the pool is not binding (it bound
in \S\ref{sec:exp-testbed}); an eviction order beating the
price blind to $p_i$, $\tau_i$ (E4); an inversion load off $\rho^*$ (E5).

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work provides analytical tools for capacity planning and scheduling
in LLM serving systems. Better utilisation of serving hardware lowers the
energy and cost of deploying language models; we see no direct negative
societal consequences beyond those generally associated with more
efficient LLM deployment.

\sloppy
Every proposition and every derived number in the paper has also been
formalised and machine-checked in Lean~4 with Mathlib; the development
is in the supplementary material. The proofs below are written for
human readers and do not follow the formal proofs step by step. The
known results quoted in \S\ref{sec:evict} (the threshold rule, the
block prefix and the guarded greedy) and the closed
form~\eqref{eq:rhostar} are verified there as well and not re-proved
here. For Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} the mean value recursion is
taken as the model, as the arrival theorem gives it; the inequalities
are proved from it.


\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:decode}]
(i) Write $a_n=1/\prod_{k\le n}\varphi(k)$, so
$L(\rho)=\sum_nna_n\rho^n/\sum_na_n\rho^n$. For $\rho_1\le\rho_2$,
\[
  L(\rho_2)-L(\rho_1)=\frac{\sum_{n,m}na_na_m(\rho_2^n\rho_1^m-\rho_1^n\rho_2^m)}{P(\rho_1)P(\rho_2)},
\]
with $P(\rho)=\sum_na_n\rho^n$. Exchanging $n$ and $m$ and averaging, the
numerator is half of
$\sum_{n,m}a_na_m(n-m)(\rho_2^n\rho_1^m-\rho_1^n\rho_2^m)$. For $n\ge m$
the last factor is $\rho_1^m\rho_2^m(\rho_2^{n-m}-\rho_1^{n-m})\ge0$, and
symmetrically for $n<m$ both factors are non-positive, so every term is
non-negative. This holds for every truncation of the sums at a finite
$N$, and $L$ is the limit of the truncated means, so it holds for $L$.
By insensitivity, $L$ depends on the policy only through $\rho$, and a
policy that adds expected demand $A$ per arrival moves the load to
$\rho+\lambda A$.

(ii) With constant capacity $\pi$ is geometric and $L=\rho/(C-\rho)$.
With $\rho'=\rho+\lambda A$ and $A=\sum_iq_i\Delta D_i$,
\[
  L(\rho')-L(\rho)=\frac{C\,\lambda A}{(C-\rho)(C-\rho')}
  =\frac{C-\rho}{C-\rho'}\cdot\frac{C\,\lambda A}{(C-\rho)^2},
\]
and the last factor is $\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi^D_i$. Since $\rho'\ge\rho$ the
prefactor is at least one.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:finite}]
Write $c=\mu Z$ and $g(q)=(1+q)/(c+1+q)$, so $Q_n=n\,g(Q_{n-1})$. The
recursion is mean value analysis for the closed network of one FIFO
server and one delay station: by the arrival theorem a turn arriving
with $n$ sessions in the system sees the stationary state with $n-1$,
so its response time is $R_n=(1+Q_{n-1})/\mu$, the throughput is
$X_n=n/(Z+R_n)$ and $Q_n=X_nR_n$. Dividing, $\rho_n=X_n/\mu=
n/(c+1+Q_{n-1})$ and $Q_n=\rho_n(1+Q_{n-1})$.

(i) $g$ is nonnegative and nondecreasing on $q\ge0$, and
$g\le1$. Hence $0\le Q_n\le n$ by induction, and
$Q_{n+1}=(n+1)g(Q_n)\ge n\,g(Q_{n-1})=Q_n$ once $Q_n\ge Q_{n-1}$,
which holds at $n=1$.

$\rho_n<1$: it is equivalent to $Q_{n-1}>n-1-c$, and $Q_n>n-c$ holds
for all $n$ by induction, since
$Q_{n+1}-(n+1-c)=c\,(Q_n+c-n)/(c+1+Q_n)>0$.

$Q_n$ is nonincreasing in $c$: $g$ is nonincreasing in $c$ at fixed
$q$, so if $Q_{n-1}(c')\le Q_{n-1}(c)$ for $c\le c'$ then
$Q_n(c')=n\,g_{c'}(Q_{n-1}(c'))\le n\,g_{c'}(Q_{n-1}(c))\le
n\,g_c(Q_{n-1}(c))=Q_n(c)$.

(ii) $W_N\le\rho_N/(\mu(1-\rho_N))$ is $Q_{N-1}(1-\rho_N)\le\rho_N$,
that is $Q_{N-1}\le\rho_N(1+Q_{N-1})=Q_N$, which is~(i).

The cap stated after the proposition: the mean number at the server of
a closed system of $N$ sessions lies in $[0,N]$ under any policy, so
$L_P'-L_P\le N-L_P$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:price} and of the remarks after it]
Write $u=1-\rho$, $m_2=\E[S^2]$,
$A=\sum_iq_i\Delta S_i$ and
$B=\sum_iq_i\bigl((S_i^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S_i^{\mathrm{hit}})^2\bigr)$.
The change raises $\E[S]$ by $A$ and $\E[S^2]$ by $B$, so the load
becomes $\rho'=\rho+\lambda A$ and $1-\rho'=u-\lambda A>0$. By
\eqref{eq:pk}, $L=\lambda^2m_2/(2u)+\rho$, hence
\[
  \Delta L=\lambda A+\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\Bigl(\frac{m_2+B}{u-\lambda A}
    -\frac{m_2}{u}\Bigr)
  =\lambda A+\frac{Y}{u(u-\lambda A)},
\]
with $Y=\lambda^2(uB+\lambda Am_2)/2\ge0$. Summing \eqref{eq:price} with
$W=\lambda m_2/(2u)$ gives $\lambda\sum_iq_i\Phi_i=\lambda A+Y/u^2$.

For the bracket, since $0<u-\lambda A\le u$, $Y/(u(u-\lambda A))\ge Y/u^2$, which is
the lower bound. For the upper bound,
\[
  \frac{u}{u-\lambda A}\Bigl(\lambda A+\frac{Y}{u^2}\Bigr)-\Delta L
  =\frac{(\lambda A)^2}{u-\lambda A}\ \ge\ 0 .
\]

For the ratios stated after the proposition, $(S^{\mathrm{miss}})^2-(S^{\mathrm{hit}})^2
=(S^{\mathrm{miss}}+S^{\mathrm{hit}})\Delta S$, so the second term of
$\Phi_i$ is $\lambda(S^{\mathrm{miss}}+S^{\mathrm{hit}})/(2u)$ times
$\Delta S_i$, and the third is $\lambda W\Delta S_i/u$. Dividing gives
both ratios.

For the divergence, let $m_1=\E[S]$ and $\lambda=(1-\varepsilon)/m_1$ with
$0<\varepsilon\le1/2$, so $\rho=1-\varepsilon$ and $\lambda\ge1/(2m_1)$.
The first two terms of $\Phi_i$ are non-negative, and the third is
$\lambda^2m_2\Delta S_i/(2\varepsilon^2)\ge m_2\Delta S_i/(8m_1^2\varepsilon^2)$,
which exceeds any bound for small~$\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:blind}]
(i) Take two programs of the same size $c>0$ that agree in everything
the rule observes, and a target $\Delta C=c$, so that either program
alone is feasible. The rule sees the same input whatever the resume
probabilities are, so it evicts a fixed one of the two, say $e$, and let
$o$ be the other. Set $p_e=1$ and $p_o=1/(|R|+2)$, both in $(0,1]$. The
evicted program costs $p_ec^2=c^2$, while $o$ costs
$c^2/(|R|+2)$, and $R\cdot c^2/(|R|+2)<c^2$ since $R<|R|+2$. The
optimum is at most the cost of $o$, so the rule's ratio exceeds~$R$.

(ii) By~\eqref{eq:prefill} the recompute work of a context of $c$
tokens is $ac+bc^2/2$; the proposition takes the quadratic term as the
price, $w_i=c_i^2$, so $w_i/c_i=c_i$ and price-per-byte order is
shortest-first (SF). SF frees $\Delta C$ whenever the pool holds it: it
stops only at a program at least as long as the remaining target or
when the pool is exhausted. For the ratio, let SF take
$t_1\le\dots\le t_m$, the first $m$ programs in sorted order. Write $L=t_m$ and $P=t_1+\dots+t_{m-1}$, so $P<\Delta C$ (SF did
not stop earlier). Let $S$ be any feasible set.

\emph{Step 1: $L^2\le\mathrm{cost}(S)$.} Every program with $c_i<L$ is
among the first $m-1$ in sorted order, and these free only $P<\Delta C$.
So $S$ contains a program with $c_i\ge L$, whose cost alone is at
least~$L^2$.

\emph{Step 2: a lower bound on $\mathrm{cost}(S)$.} Let
$f(x)=x(L-x)$ for $x<L$ and $f(x)=0$ otherwise, so $f\ge0$ on $[0,\infty)$
and $x^2\ge Lx-f(x)$ for all $x\ge0$ (with equality for $x<L$, and
$x^2\ge Lx$ for $x\ge L$). Summing over $S$ and using
$\sum_{i\in S}c_i\ge\Delta C$,
\[
  \mathrm{cost}(S)\ \ge\ L\,\Delta C-\sum_{i\in S}f(c_i)
  \ \ge\ L\,\Delta C-\sum_{j<m}t_j(L-t_j),
\]
because only programs with $c_i<L$ contribute to the sum, all of them
are among $t_1,\dots,t_{m-1}$, and $t_j(L-t_j)\ge0$ for $j<m$. The right
side equals $L(\Delta C-P)+\sum_{j<m}t_j^2$.

\emph{Step 3.} Since $\Delta C-P>0$,
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{cost}(\mathrm{SF})&=\textstyle\sum_{j<m}t_j^2+L^2\\
  &\le \mathrm{cost}(S)-L(\Delta C-P)+L^2\\
  &\le \mathrm{cost}(S)+L^2,
\end{align*}
and Step~1 bounds the last term by $\mathrm{cost}(S)$.

Tightness: on $K,K+1$ with $\Delta C=K+1$, SF takes $K$ (leaving $1$), then
$K+1$, at cost $K^2+(K+1)^2$, while $\{K+1\}$ costs $(K+1)^2$.
\end{proof}
